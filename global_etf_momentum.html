Risk level

How modes are assigned

Each ETF’s weekly total-return price is divided by the benchmark to get its relative strength. Two readings are derived from it, both centred on 100.

◤ RecoveringRS-Ratio below 100, RS-Momentum 100 or above
▲ AcceleratingRS-Ratio 100 or above, RS-Momentum 100 or above
▼ UnderperformingRS-Ratio below 100, RS-Momentum below 100
◢ DeceleratingRS-Ratio 100 or above, RS-Momentum below 100
Relative strength = 100 × ETF ÷ benchmark · RS-Ratio = 100 × 3-week EMA ÷ 10-week average of relative strength · RS-Momentum = 100 + change in RS-Ratio over 3 weeks

ETFs typically rotate clockwise: Recovering, then Accelerating, then Decelerating, then Underperforming. Readings within 1 point of the centre are treated as noise and carry low confidence.

Before you invest

  • Your country of residence taxes these gains — Indian tax applies only if you are an Indian tax resident
  • Holdings above US$60,000 can face US estate tax for non-US persons; see the FAQ below
  • Some brokers do not onboard residents of every country — confirm eligibility first

NISA growth quota Tax-free in Japan

Japan residents, including NRIs living in Japan, can hold US-listed ETFs inside NISA through a Japanese securities account.

  • US-listed ETFs are bought within the growth investment quota (成長投資枠), up to ¥2.4 million a year and ¥12 million lifetime
  • Gains and dividends are free of Japanese tax inside NISA
  • US withholding tax of 10% on dividends still applies and cannot be reclaimed inside NISA

Choosing a Japanese broker

  • Major online brokers such as SBI Securities, Rakuten Securities and Monex offer US-listed ETFs in NISA
  • Compare currency-exchange spreads and per-trade commissions — several brokers waive US ETF commissions for NISA
  • Only one NISA account is allowed per person at a time

Questions

Is the data on this page accurate?

After every US trading session, prices, returns, risk, liquidity and fund facts (expense ratio, fund size, yield, inception) are refreshed. Two independent checks run before anything is published:

  • Accuracy audit — every ETF is checked for valid prices, the price sitting inside its 52-week range, recomputed returns, realistic volatility and drawdown, expense ratios in a valid range, a complete weekly history, and day-over-day continuity against the previous publish. If any critical check fails, the update is held back and the previous day’s data stays online.
  • Engine check — when the page loads, it recomputes every momentum mode against the S&P 500 and compares it with the published reference. The result is shown at the top of the page.

Prices are end-of-day in US dollars and total-return (dividends reinvested) for returns and momentum. Always confirm the live price on your broker before placing an order.

Accelerating, Decelerating, Recovering or Underperforming — what do they mean for me?
  • ▲ Accelerating — beating the benchmark and gaining strength: the strongest current trend, but it can be late in the move. Buy in parts and watch for a shift to Decelerating.
  • ◤ Recovering — still behind but improving: an early turn with higher reward and higher risk. Wait for two or three weeks of confirmation.
  • ◢ Decelerating — still ahead but fading: hold or book partial profits rather than add new money.
  • ▼ Underperforming — behind and weakening: avoid for momentum.

Modes describe past relative performance. Broad core ETFs can be bought regularly whatever their mode.

Which benchmark should I use?

Use the S&P 500 for US equity ETFs, the Nasdaq-100 to judge growth and technology funds, the Russell 2000 for small caps, MSCI ACWI for a global view, Developed ex-US or Emerging Markets for country funds, US Aggregate Bond for bond ETFs and Gold for precious metals. Comparing a fund with its own parent market shows whether it leads within that market.

What does Confidence mean?

Four checks cross-examine each mode: whether the ETF actually beat the benchmark over 13 weeks on the side its RS-Ratio shows, whether its last 4 weeks of relative return improved on the 4 weeks before (matching RS-Momentum), whether it trades at least $10 million a day, and whether its reading sits outside the noise band. High = all four agree, Medium = three, Low = two or fewer.

What do the Trend S·M·L chips and roles mean?

S (short term) compares price with its 10-week average and the 13-week return; M (medium) with its 26-week average and 26-week return; L (long) with its 52-week average, whether that average is rising, and the 1-year return. Green = positive, red = negative, grey = mixed.

Roles: Core (broad markets — most long-term money), Factor (growth, value, dividend, momentum, quality, low volatility), Sector, Thematic, Country, Bond, Commodity and Digital (spot bitcoin and ether). Sector, thematic, country and digital funds are usually held as smaller satellites.

Why is an ETF “Not classified”?

At least 20 weeks of clean trading history are needed. Recently launched funds are listed but not classified, to avoid misleading signals.

What is the US estate-tax risk for non-US investors?

For people who are neither US citizens nor US residents, US-listed ETFs are US-situs assets. If their total value exceeds US$60,000 at death, the estate can owe US estate tax of up to 40%, and India has no estate-tax treaty with the US. Investors with larger holdings often consider Ireland-domiciled UCITS ETFs listed in London, which are generally outside US estate tax and suffer 15% US dividend withholding at fund level. Japan’s treaty with the US provides some relief for Japan residents. Take professional advice for large portfolios.

US-listed ETFs or Indian international funds — which is better?

Indian-listed international ETFs and fund-of-funds need no LRS remittance, but industry overseas limits often push international ETFs to trade well above their NAV, and new investments can be paused. US-listed ETFs trade close to their value at very low cost, but need a remittance, US-dollar exposure and extra tax reporting. Compare both on the India ETF Momentum page.

How current is the data?

End-of-day closes, refreshed after the US market close every trading day. The latest week runs up to the date shown at the top. Fund facts are refreshed daily and can lag the issuer by a few days.

How does the Global Portfolio Simulator choose ETFs?

Each plan starts from an asset mix for your goal and risk level — equity, US investment-grade bonds, gold and Treasury bills. Equity is split between US large and total-market funds, US small and mid caps, developed markets outside the US, emerging markets and, for goals of 3 years or more, a small factor satellite.

Every slot has a short list of ETFs doing the same job. Each must pass up to nine checks: in today’s audited data, at least 3 years of returns, traded at least $20 million a day, fund size of at least $1 billion, expense ratio under the slot’s cap, 1-year return close to similar ETFs, volatility no more than 1.3 times similar ETFs, a clean year of weekly prices and, for the factor slot, an Accelerating mode against the S&P 500 with Medium or High confidence. Survivors are ranked on cost 35% · liquidity 25% · size 20% · track record 10% · consistency with peers 10%.

Market-condition overlay: for goals of 3 years or more, when the S&P 500’s long-term trend turns negative (below a falling 52-week average with a negative 1-year return), 10 points move from equity into bonds, trimmed evenly across the equity slots. The money moves back when the trend recovers. The “Why these ETFs” tab shows every check with its figure.

Is the “Risk & behaviour” view a forecast?

No. It replays how the exact model mix moved over the last 60 weeks of audited weekly prices, rebalanced to its target weights each week, and compares it with the S&P 500. Past behaviour over a short window is an illustration only and does not predict future returns.
