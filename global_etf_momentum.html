Risk level

How modes are assigned

Each ETF’s weekly total-return price is divided by the benchmark to get its relative strength. Two readings are derived from it, both centred on 100.

◤ RecoveringRS-Ratio below 100, RS-Momentum 100 or above
▲ AcceleratingRS-Ratio 100 or above, RS-Momentum 100 or above
▼ UnderperformingRS-Ratio below 100, RS-Momentum below 100
◢ DeceleratingRS-Ratio 100 or above, RS-Momentum below 100
Relative strength = 100 × ETF ÷ benchmark · RS-Ratio = 100 × 3-week EMA ÷ 10-week average of relative strength · RS-Momentum = 100 + change in RS-Ratio over 3 weeks

ETFs typically rotate clockwise: Recovering, then Accelerating, then Decelerating, then Underperforming. Readings within 1 point of the centre are treated as noise and carry low confidence.

Tax & rules by country

How US-listed ETFs are taxed and reported depends on where you are tax-resident — not on your nationality. Choose your situation.

Sending moneyLiberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS): up to US$250,000 per person per financial year for overseas investment. LRS money cannot be used for margin or leveraged trading abroad. Your bank collects 20% TCS on investment remittances above ₹10 lakh in a financial year — it is adjusted against your income tax or refunded when you file your return.
DividendsThe US withholds 25% under the India–US tax treaty once you file Form W-8BEN with your broker (30% without it). The dividend is also taxed in India at your income slab; the US tax is claimed as a foreign tax credit by filing Form 67 before your return.
Capital gainsThe US generally does not tax non-US investors on gains. In India, US-listed ETFs held for more than 24 months are long-term: 12.5% (no indexation); held 24 months or less: taxed at your income slab. Surcharge and 4% cess apply.
ReportingReport every foreign holding in Schedule FA (calendar-year basis, January–December), foreign income in Schedule FSI and the tax credit in Schedule TR, with Form 67. Missing foreign assets can attract penalties under the Black Money Act.
Estate taxUS-listed ETFs are US assets. For non-US persons the US estate tax applies to US assets above US$60,000, and there is no India–US estate tax treaty. Some investors use Ireland-domiciled (UCITS) ETFs or Indian international funds to avoid this exposure.
NISA (since 2024)Two tax-free quotas: the growth investment quota (成長投資枠) up to ¥2.4 million a year and the tsumitate quota (つみたて投資枠) up to ¥1.2 million a year — ¥3.6 million in total. Lifetime limit ¥18 million at purchase cost, of which at most ¥12 million in the growth quota. Quota used by holdings you sell is restored from the following year, and the tax-free period has no end date. One NISA account per person, for residents aged 18 or over.

Gains and dividends are free of Japanese tax, but the US still withholds 10% on dividends under the Japan–US treaty, and that tax cannot be credited inside NISA. Losses inside NISA cannot be offset against other gains.
Outside NISAIn a specific account (特定口座) gains and dividends are taxed at 20.315% (15.315% national tax including the reconstruction surtax + 5% local). Bond and T-bill ETFs, whose return is mostly dividends, lose more to the unrecoverable withholding inside NISA — some investors hold them outside NISA.
Estate taxUS assets of non-US persons can face US estate tax; the Japan–US estate tax treaty gives a pro-rated exemption that is often larger than the basic US$60,000. Japanese inheritance tax also applies.
Who to useA Japanese securities company registered with the Financial Services Agency (FSA). Compare currency-exchange spreads and commissions — several brokers waive US ETF commissions inside NISA. See the NISA guide below for every rule.
Who this coversUS citizens, green-card holders and US tax residents — including Indian citizens (NRIs) living and working in the US.
Capital gainsHeld one year or less: taxed as ordinary income. Held more than one year: 0%, 15% or 20% by income, plus the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax above US$200,000 (single) / US$250,000 (married filing jointly), plus state income tax in most states.
DividendsQualified dividends are taxed at the long-term capital-gains rates; other distributions (most bond and T-bill ETF income) at ordinary rates. Interest from US Treasuries is exempt from state income tax.
Wash-sale ruleA loss is disallowed if you buy a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale — it is added to the cost of the new shares instead.
FormsForm W-9 to your broker (no withholding), Forms 1099-DIV and 1099-B at year end. Bank or investment accounts outside the US (for example in India) may need an FBAR (over US$10,000 in total at any time) and Form 8938 (FATCA).
Indian fundsIndian mutual funds and ETFs are usually PFICs for US tax — yearly Form 8621 and punitive treatment without special elections. US-listed ETFs avoid this. India taxes non-residents only on income arising in India, so US-listed ETF gains of a US-resident NRI are not taxed in India.
Capital gainsThe US generally does not tax capital gains of non-US investors (exceptions include people present in the US for 183 days or more in the year). Your home country taxes them under its own rules.
DividendsThe US withholds 30% unless a tax-treaty rate applies — file Form W-8BEN with your broker; it stays valid until the end of the third calendar year after you sign it.
Estate taxUS assets above US$60,000 can face US estate tax for non-US persons unless an estate-tax treaty applies.
Europe & UKRetail investors in the EU and UK usually cannot buy US-listed ETFs because they do not publish an EU/UK key information document (PRIIPs); Ireland- or Luxembourg-domiciled UCITS ETFs tracking the same indices are used instead.

Questions

Is the data on this page accurate?

After every US trading session, prices, returns, risk, liquidity and fund facts (expense ratio, fund size, yield, inception) are refreshed. Two independent checks run before anything is published:

  • Accuracy audit — every ETF is checked for valid prices, the price sitting inside its 52-week range, recomputed returns, realistic volatility and drawdown, expense ratios in a valid range, a complete weekly history, and day-over-day continuity against the previous publish. If any critical check fails, the update is held back and the previous day’s data stays online.
  • Engine check — when the page loads, it recomputes every momentum mode against the S&P 500 and compares it with the published reference. The result is shown at the top of the page.

Prices are end-of-day in US dollars and total-return (dividends reinvested) for returns and momentum. Always confirm the live price on your broker before placing an order.

Accelerating, Decelerating, Recovering or Underperforming — what do they mean for me?
  • ▲ Accelerating — beating the benchmark and gaining strength: the strongest current trend, though it can be late in the move; momentum investors often spread purchases and watch for a shift to Decelerating.
  • ◤ Recovering — still behind but improving: an early turn with higher reward and higher risk; momentum investors often wait for two or three weeks of confirmation.
  • ◢ Decelerating — still ahead but fading: momentum investors often hold or trim rather than add new money.
  • ▼ Underperforming — behind and weakening: the weakest mode for momentum.

Modes describe past relative performance. Broad core ETFs can be bought regularly whatever their mode.

Which benchmark should I use?

Use the S&P 500 for US equity ETFs, the Nasdaq-100 to judge growth and technology funds, the Russell 2000 for small caps, MSCI ACWI for a global view, Developed ex-US or Emerging Markets for country funds, US Aggregate Bond for bond ETFs and Gold for precious metals. Comparing a fund with its own parent market shows whether it leads within that market.

What does Confidence mean?

Four checks cross-examine each mode: whether the ETF actually beat the benchmark over 13 weeks on the side its RS-Ratio shows, whether its last 4 weeks of relative return improved on the 4 weeks before (matching RS-Momentum), whether it trades at least $10 million a day, and whether its reading sits outside the noise band. High = all four agree, Medium = three, Low = two or fewer.

What do the Trend S·M·L chips and roles mean?

S (short term) compares price with its 10-week average and the 13-week return; M (medium) with its 26-week average and 26-week return; L (long) with its 52-week average, whether that average is rising, and the 1-year return. Green = positive, red = negative, grey = mixed.

Roles: Core (broad markets — most long-term money), Factor (growth, value, dividend, momentum, quality, low volatility), Sector, Thematic, Country, Bond, Commodity and Digital (spot bitcoin and ether). Sector, thematic, country and digital funds are usually held as smaller satellites.

Why is an ETF “Not classified”?

At least 20 weeks of clean trading history are needed. Recently launched funds are listed but not classified, to avoid misleading signals.

What is the US estate-tax risk for non-US investors?

For people who are neither US citizens nor US residents, US-listed ETFs are US-situs assets. If their total value exceeds US$60,000 at death, the estate can owe US estate tax of up to 40%, and India has no estate-tax treaty with the US. Investors with larger holdings often consider Ireland-domiciled UCITS ETFs listed in London, which are generally outside US estate tax and suffer 15% US dividend withholding at fund level. Japan’s treaty with the US provides some relief for Japan residents. Take professional advice for large portfolios.

US-listed ETFs or Indian international funds — which is better?

Indian-listed international ETFs and fund-of-funds need no LRS remittance, but industry overseas limits often push international ETFs to trade well above their NAV, and new investments can be paused. US-listed ETFs trade close to their value at very low cost, but need a remittance, US-dollar exposure and extra tax reporting. Compare both on the India ETF Momentum page.

What is NISA, and can I hold US-listed ETFs in it?

NISA (少額投資非課税制度) is Japan's tax-free investment account for residents aged 18 or over. Since 2024 it has a growth investment quota of ¥2.4 million a year and a tsumitate quota of ¥1.2 million a year, with a lifetime limit of ¥18 million (at most ¥12 million in the growth quota) and no end date for the tax-free period. US-listed ETFs can be bought only within the growth quota, through a Japanese securities company that offers them. Gains and dividends are then free of Japanese tax.

Do I still pay US tax on dividends inside NISA?

Yes. The US withholds 10% on dividends for Japan residents under the Japan–US tax treaty, and inside NISA that tax cannot be credited back because no Japanese tax is due. ETFs that pay most of their return as dividends — bond and T-bill ETFs — lose the most this way, so some investors keep them in a specific account (特定口座), where the foreign tax credit can be claimed, and use NISA for growth-oriented equity ETFs. Losses inside NISA also cannot be offset against other gains.

Which quota should a Japan resident use for this simulator's model portfolio?

The simulator's ETFs are US-listed, so in NISA they all fall in the growth investment quota (up to ¥2.4 million a year). The tsumitate quota is for designated investment trusts — many Japanese index funds track the same broad indices (for example the S&P 500 or MSCI ACWI) and can be bought there in yen, without currency conversion. Choose "Japan resident (NISA)" under "Where you are tax-resident" in the simulator to see the related notes.

How are US persons taxed on ETFs?

For US citizens, green-card holders and US tax residents: gains on ETFs held more than one year are taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% by income, shorter holdings at ordinary income rates; the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax applies above US$200,000 (single) or US$250,000 (married filing jointly); qualified dividends get the long-term rates; and most states add their own income tax. The wash-sale rule disallows a loss if a substantially identical security is bought within 30 days before or after the sale.

I am an NRI living in the US — what changes for me?

For US tax you are a US person: you file Form W-9 (no withholding) and pay US tax on gains and dividends as above. India taxes non-residents only on income arising in India, so US-listed ETF gains are not taxed in India. Your Indian accounts may need to be reported on an FBAR and Form 8938, and Indian mutual funds are usually PFICs with heavy US reporting — one reason US-based NRIs often prefer US-listed ETFs. Several Indian fund houses do not accept US-resident investors at all.

What is Form W-8BEN and how long is it valid?

Form W-8BEN tells your broker that you are not a US person and claims your country's tax-treaty rate on US dividends (for example 25% for India, 10% for Japan, instead of 30%). It stays valid until the end of the third calendar year after you sign it — most brokers remind you to renew. US persons file Form W-9 instead.

Does the US tax capital gains of non-US investors?

Generally no. Gains on US-listed ETFs sold by non-resident investors are usually not taxed by the US (exceptions include people present in the US for 183 days or more in the year, or gains connected with a US business). Your country of residence taxes them under its own rules — for example India (12.5% after 24 months, otherwise slab rate) or Japan (20.315% outside NISA, tax-free inside NISA).

How do I report US-listed ETFs in my Indian tax return?

Resident Indians report every foreign holding in Schedule FA — for the calendar year (January–December) ending in the financial year — even if nothing was sold. Dividends go in Schedule FSI, the US tax withheld in Schedule TR, and Form 67 must be filed before the return to claim the foreign tax credit. Capital gains are reported in Schedule CG. Missing foreign assets can lead to penalties under the Black Money Act, so many investors use a chartered accountant for their first year.

Why can't I buy US-listed ETFs from Europe or the UK?

EU and UK rules (PRIIPs) require a key information document for funds sold to retail investors, and most US ETF issuers do not publish one, so EU and UK brokers usually block US-listed ETFs for retail clients. Ireland- or Luxembourg-domiciled UCITS ETFs that track the same indices are the usual alternative.

Can a resident Indian buy US-listed ETFs?

Yes, under the RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), which allows up to US$250,000 per person per financial year. The usual route: open an international brokerage account (or a GIFT City / NSE IX account), complete its KYC with PAN and address proof, send money from your bank under LRS (Form A2, purpose code for overseas portfolio investment), and buy in US dollars. You cannot use a credit card or borrowed money for LRS investments. Holdings must be reported in your Indian tax return (Schedule FA), and foreign tax paid is claimed with Form 67.

Is TCS a cost? Can I get it back?

Tax collected at source (20% of the amount above ₹10 lakh remitted for investment in a financial year) is not an extra tax: it appears in your Form 26AS / AIS and is adjusted against your income tax, or refunded when you file your return. It does tie up cash until then. Remittances up to ₹10 lakh a year have no TCS.

How does the rupee affect returns on US ETFs?

Your return in rupees is roughly the ETF's dollar return plus the change in the US dollar against the rupee. Over long periods the rupee has tended to weaken against the dollar, which has added to rupee returns, but in some years it strengthens and reduces them. Conversion fees and the bank's exchange-rate margin are a cost on the way in and out. Figures on this page are in US dollars.

US-listed ETFs or Ireland-domiciled (UCITS) ETFs?

Many international investors also use Ireland-domiciled UCITS ETFs listed in London or Europe. Commonly cited differences: US dividends inside an Irish fund are generally withheld at 15% at the fund level (versus 25% on a US-listed ETF for Indian residents), and Irish-domiciled funds are generally not treated as US assets for US estate tax for non-US persons. US-listed ETFs are usually cheaper, larger and far more liquid. Availability depends on your broker. Tax treatment can change — confirm with a tax professional.

Do I need a lot of money? What about fractional shares?

No. Many international brokers allow fractional shares, so even US$10–50 can buy part of an ETF priced at hundreds of dollars. Without fractional shares, a small amount may buy one share only every few purchases — the Portfolio Simulator shows this when the "fractional shares" box is unticked. Check each broker's minimum order, commission and currency-conversion fee, which matter more for small amounts.

What is the Very aggressive model, and how are the return figures worked out?

Very aggressive aims at the highest long-run growth the model allows: 95% in equities and 5% in gold from a 5-year goal (85% equities, 5% bonds and 10% gold for 3–5 years), with a US large-growth slot (Nasdaq-100 and US growth ETFs) and larger emerging-market and factor shares. Expect the deepest falls of the four levels — 30–50% in a severe bear market — and years of waiting to recover.

  • Past return of this mix: today's ETFs held in today's weights over the last 1, 3 and 5 years (dividends reinvested) — a description of the past, not a forecast.
  • Historical model estimate: for equities, the average of the index's earnings yield plus 2.5 points of long-run growth and the ETF's own 5-year record (kept between 3% and 12%), after costs; bonds and Treasury bills use their current yield; gold 5%.
  • After-tax value: your amount and frequency over the goal's years at that estimate, after the dividend tax and, for resident Indians, 12.5% (+ cess) on gains held 24 months or more and 30% on newer purchases; inside Japan's NISA gains are tax-free; NRIs see the value before tax in their country. "Today's money" removes 2.5% a year of US-dollar inflation.

"Compare every risk level" shows the same figures for all four levels side by side. Higher estimates come with deeper falls; none of these figures is a promise.

How are the Simulator Buy trades priced?
  • Market-on-close: every buy executes at the official closing price (4:00 pm New York time) of its plan date — or of the next session the ETF trades if the US market is closed.
  • Fractional shares in steps of 0.0001, after a commission of $0.0035 a share (at least $0.35, at most 1% of the trade). Cents left over, or an order under $1, wait for that slot’s next buy. Shares are credited the next US business day (T+1).
  • Dividends: shares bought before the ex-date earn the dividend; 25% US tax is withheld for India residents (India–US treaty), nothing at source for the US-based team members. The cash joins the slot’s next buy.
  • Never sells: when the model replaces an ETF, new money goes to the new choice and earlier shares are kept.

What are futures and options (F&O)?

They are derivatives — contracts whose value comes from another asset such as an index or an ETF. A future is an agreement to buy or sell at a fixed price on a set date; gains and losses are settled every day. An option gives the buyer the right, but not the duty, to buy (call) or sell (put) at a fixed price until expiry, for an upfront price called the premium. Both are leveraged: a small amount of money controls a much larger position, so prices of the contracts move several times faster than the ETF itself, and they expire.

Why are futures and options not suitable for beginners?
  • Leverage cuts both ways — a 2% move in the index can mean a 20–50% gain or loss on the contract; futures and sold options can lose more than the money you put in.
  • Time works against option buyers — an option loses value every day as expiry nears (time decay), so you must be right on direction, size and timing.
  • Costs and spreads are larger relative to the money involved, and frequent trading adds tax and charges.
  • The evidence: India's market regulator SEBI found that about 93% of individual F&O traders lost money between FY2022 and FY2024. Studies in other markets show similar results for retail traders.
  • Behaviour: fast-moving positions encourage over-trading and emotional decisions — the opposite of a long-term plan.

For long-term wealth, a low-cost, diversified ETF portfolio held for years has a far better record. Learn derivatives only with money you can afford to lose, after the core portfolio is in place.

Can options protect a global ETF portfolio? How does risk management with options work?

Professionals sometimes hedge with options on broad ETFs such as SPY or QQQ. The common structures:

  • Protective put — buy a put option below today's price (for example 10% lower, 3–6 months out). If the market falls further, the put gains and offsets part of the loss. It works like insurance: the premium (often 1–3% for a few months) is lost if the market does not fall, so doing it all the time costs a lot over the years.
  • Collar — buy a put and pay for it by selling a call above today's price. The downside is limited, but so is the upside.
  • Covered call — sell a call against ETF shares you own to earn the premium. It adds income in flat markets but caps gains in a rally and gives little protection in a big fall.
  • Size the hedge — the number of contracts must match the value and beta of what you hold (one US equity option usually covers 100 shares).

For most long-term investors, simpler tools protect better and cost nothing: the right asset mix (bonds, T-bills and gold alongside equities), diversification across regions, an emergency fund so you never sell in a crash, and rebalancing once a year. That is how this Portfolio Simulator manages risk — it never uses derivatives. Note: resident Indians generally cannot use money sent abroad under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme for margin or derivatives trading, and many brokers outside the US restrict US options for retail clients — check the rules where you live.

What is algo trading (algorithmic trading)?

Algo trading means a computer program places orders automatically when pre-set rules are met — for example "buy when the 20-day average crosses above the 50-day average" — instead of a person clicking each order. Large institutions use algorithms mainly to split big orders and reduce trading costs; high-frequency firms trade in microseconds.

  • Regulation: in India, SEBI's framework for retail algo trading (rolled out from 2025) lets investors run algorithms only through their broker's approved channel — each algo is identified to the exchange, and algo providers must be empanelled with the exchanges. In the US and Japan, algorithms run through a broker's API under that broker's risk controls.
  • Risks: strategies that looked great in back-tests often fail live (over-fitting), a coding error or bad data can place wrong orders in seconds, and costs add up with frequent trades.
  • Scams: be very careful with apps, Telegram groups or "bots" promising guaranteed or fixed monthly returns — regulators repeatedly warn about them.
