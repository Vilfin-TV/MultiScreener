SIF: India's New ₹10 Lakh Investment Class — All 33 Funds, Every Strategy, Every Risk Explained

August 25, 2026

It was September 30, 2025 — a date Indian asset managers had circled on their calendars for months. On that day, India formally launched a new investment product category that had been debated, drafted, and delayed since 2023. The first Specialised Investment Funds (SIFs) opened their NFO windows, offering something Indian finance had never officially permitted before: a regulated, SEBI-supervised fund that could simultaneously bet for and against the stock market.

Within seven months, 33 schemes from 17 fund houses had launched. Total assets under management hit ₹13,182 crore by May 2026 — a 6.5x jump from ₹2,010 crore at inception. Every fund eligible for six-month evaluation had beaten its benchmark. JioBlackRock joined in July 2026. More AMCs are queuing at SEBI's door.

Something new has arrived in Indian investing. Here is everything you need to know about it.

First: What Is a Specialised Investment Fund?

Think of the Indian investment landscape as a road with two lanes. One lane is the Mutual Fund highway — open to everyone from ₹100, tightly regulated, no fancy manoeuvres allowed. The other lane is the Portfolio Management Service (PMS) expressway — minimum ₹50 lakh, each client gets a custom portfolio, the manager can go wherever they want.

Between these two lanes was a gap. Millions of serious Indian investors — HNIs, professionals, experienced market participants — had too much money to be satisfied by rigid mutual fund categories, but not enough to park ₹50 lakh in a PMS. SEBI noticed this gap in 2023. In December 2024, it amended the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 1996. On February 27, 2025, it released the formal framework (Circular SEBI/HO/IMD/IMD-I POD-1/P/CIR/2025/26). From April 1, 2025, the SIF lane opened.

A Specialised Investment Fund is a pooled investment product — legally housed inside an AMC's existing mutual fund trust structure — that is permitted to use advanced strategies not available to regular MF schemes. The big ones: going short on stocks (betting they will fall), using derivatives not just for hedging but for returns, and concentrating aggressively in sectors or maturities.

It is still regulated like a mutual fund. NAV is published. SEBI oversees it. But the strategies it can run look much closer to a hedge fund.

The ₹10 Lakh Rule — And the Fine Print

The minimum investment in any SIF is ₹10 lakh per PAN, across all SIF strategies offered by one AMC. This is the most misunderstood rule about SIFs.

SEBI's April 2025 clarification circular cleared it up: the ₹10 lakh threshold is aggregated at the PAN level across an AMC's entire SIF offering — not per individual scheme. Meaning: if you put ₹6 lakh in ICICI Prudential's iSIF Hybrid Long-Short and ₹4 lakh in their iSIF Equity Long-Short, you have met the ₹10 lakh minimum. You do not need ₹10 lakh in each scheme separately.

SIP is permitted under SIF — but your aggregate SIP contributions must add up to ₹10 lakh before the account is considered fully activated.

Accredited Investor exception: If you qualify as an SEBI Accredited Investor, the ₹10 lakh floor does not apply. SEBI's accredited investor criteria (meeting any one):

  • Annual income of ₹2 crore or more, or
  • Net worth of ₹7.5 crore+ with at least ₹3.75 crore in financial assets, or
  • Annual income of ₹1 crore+ and net worth of ₹5 crore+ with ₹2.5 crore in financial assets

(Your primary residence is excluded from the net worth calculation.)

Who Can Invest?

SIF is not restricted to HNIs or institutions — it is legally open to any Indian investor who meets the ₹10 lakh threshold. In practice, eligible investors include:

  • Resident individuals and NRIs
  • Hindu Undivided Families (HUFs)
  • Family trusts and sole proprietorships
  • Institutional investors (banks, insurance companies, corporates)

The ₹10 lakh floor effectively makes this a product for upper-middle-class investors and above — but the door is not locked to retail participants who can mobilize that capital.

The 7 SEBI-Approved SIF Strategies

SEBI defines exactly seven permitted investment strategies across three buckets. An AMC may launch one scheme per strategy category. Here is each one explained:

Equity-Oriented Strategies (3)

1. Equity Long-Short Fund — Minimum 80% in equities. Buys undervalued stocks, simultaneously shorts overvalued ones via futures and options. Maximum 25% unhedged short exposure. Daily redemption. Think of this as a mutual fund that can also profit when markets fall — if the manager gets the short calls right.

2. Equity Ex-Top 100 Long-Short Fund — Same long-short structure but focused on mid-cap and small-cap stocks (outside the top 100 companies by market cap). Minimum 65% invested outside the Nifty 100 universe. These are higher-volatility, higher-upside bets with a short hedge overlay. Daily redemption.

3. Sector Rotation Long-Short Fund — Concentrates in a maximum of 4 sectors at any one time. Goes long on sectors showing momentum, shorts sectors in decline. Minimum 80% in equities. This is the most aggressive equity strategy SEBI permits. Daily redemption.

Debt-Oriented Strategies (2)

4. Debt Long-Short Fund — Takes positions across the bond maturity spectrum and credit quality ladder. Uses interest rate derivatives to hedge or profit from rate movements. Single issuer cap: 20% for AAA, 16% for AA, 12% for A and below. A single sector capped at 25% of NAV. This is a bond fund with a trader's toolkit.

5. Debt Sectoral Long-Short Fund — Invests across at least 2 debt sectors with a single-sector ceiling of 75% of the portfolio. More concentrated than the standard Debt L/S. No live schemes launched in this category as of August 2026.

Hybrid Strategies (2)

6. Active Asset Allocator Long-Short Fund — The most flexible SIF permitted. Dynamically shifts allocation across equities, debt, derivatives, REITs, InvITs, and commodities. Has interval-based (not daily) redemption — investors can exit only at scheduled windows. Rated "High" risk. Fewer redemption windows mean the manager can take longer-term positions without worrying about daily outflows.

7. Hybrid Long-Short Fund — Minimum 25% each in equity and debt, combining long equity positions with short overlays and fixed income. Also uses interval-based redemption. This is the most popular SIF category so far — 11 of the 33 live schemes fall here. Rated "Moderate" to "High" risk depending on the AMC.

Read the Sharpe ratio, not just the headline return

Tata Titanium returned only 1.22% while taking on 8.66% volatility — its Sharpe of -0.37 means it is generating less return than a risk-free instrument (like a liquid fund) for every unit of risk. Meanwhile, Kotak Infinity returned 1.98% with just 1.90% volatility and a Sharpe of 7.67 — extraordinarily efficient. ABSL Apex (Sharpe 3.88) and HSBC RedHex (4.18) also stand out on this metric. Raw return numbers alone mislead here — a 1.98% return with a 7.67 Sharpe is a stronger result than a 9.19% return with a 1.03 Sharpe, because the former is nearly free of risk while the latter required significant volatility to achieve it.

For Equity Long-Short funds, Quant's qSIF posted a 3-month return of +19.17% (TER: 0.98%) and its Equity Long-Short returned +15.47% (TER: 0.95%) as of July 2026, per Groww data. These extraordinary numbers coincide with a sharp mid-cap rally — the real test will come when the cycle turns.

The correction of late 2025 through early 2026 tested short-side positioning, and the arbitrage-oriented managers posted their best relative numbers during that period.

Risk — What Can Actually Go Wrong

Strategy Risks

  • Unlimited short-side losses — if a shorted stock rises sharply, losses on that position can exceed the original stake
  • Sector concentration — Sector Rotation SIFs are in ≤4 sectors at a time. A sudden regulatory shock to one sector can devastate the fund
  • Mid/small-cap illiquidity — Ex-Top 100 funds carry significant liquidity risk in a market downturn
  • Manager-specific risk — long-short strategies live or die on the manager's ability to identify both winners and losers. No benchmark tracking safety net

Structural Risks

  • Interval redemption lock-in — Hybrid and Active Asset Allocator SIFs have scheduled windows, not daily exits. You cannot leave when markets fall if it is not a redemption day
  • Higher single-issuer debt caps — Debt SIFs can hold 20% in a single AAA issuer (vs. standard MF limit of 10%) — more concentrated credit risk
  • No track record — all 33 funds launched in 2025–2026. No long-term performance data exists
  • Derivative amplification — derivatives are used for returns, not just hedging. Losses can compound in volatile markets

Who Should — and Should Not — Invest

SIF is suited for investors who...

  • Have ₹10 lakh+ available over and above emergency funds and core MF/equity SIP portfolio
  • Have invested in equity MFs for 5+ years and understand market cycles
  • Seek uncorrelated returns — strategies that can profit in bear markets
  • Can tolerate interval redemption structures (for Hybrid/Active Asset Allocator SIFs)
  • Want hedge fund-like strategy access at MF-level costs and oversight

SIF is NOT suited for investors who...

  • Are investing for the first time or have less than 3 years of equity market experience
  • Need complete liquidity at all times
  • Are deploying this ₹10 lakh as their only or primary investment corpus
  • Expect guaranteed or fixed returns
  • Are chasing 3-month return headlines without understanding the underlying strategy

Three Things Not to Confuse About SIFs

1. "₹10 lakh per scheme" vs "₹10 lakh per PAN"

The ₹10 lakh minimum is NOT per scheme — it is the aggregate across all SIF strategies of a single AMC, tracked at your PAN level. SEBI clarified this in April 2025. If you split ₹6 lakh and ₹4 lakh across two schemes from the same AMC, you have met the threshold.

2. "SIF uses derivatives" ≠ "SIF is a leveraged fund"

SEBI caps unhedged short exposure at 25% of the portfolio. SIF is not an F&O speculation product. It uses derivatives strategically within defined limits — not for 5x or 10x leverage. Comparing it to a leveraged ETF or an F&O trader's account is inaccurate.

3. "SIF" vs "AIF Category III" — they are not the same

Both use long-short strategies. But AIF Category III requires a minimum of ₹1 crore, is regulated under SEBI's AIF regulations (not MF regulations), and does not publish daily NAV. AIF Cat III is taxed at slab rates — not at the preferential 12.5% LTCG available to equity-oriented SIFs. SIF gives you similar strategy access for one-tenth the minimum, with daily NAV transparency and MF-level regulatory oversight.

Taxation: More Nuanced Than It Looks

SIF taxation follows the pass-through structure of mutual funds — tax applies only when you redeem, not at the fund level. The fund itself is tax-exempt under Section 10(23D). But within Hybrid SIFs, the actual tax treatment differs by fund depending on how much equity the scheme holds.

Do You Actually Need a SIF?

Here is the honest answer: probably not yet — and that is not a criticism of the product.

If your mutual fund SIP portfolio is growing steadily, your emergency fund is solid, and you have not yet maxed out equity MF exposure across large-cap, mid-cap, and flexicap categories — a SIF adds complexity before it adds value. The core case for SIF is sophisticated strategy access: uncorrelated returns, bear market hedging, mid-cap alpha capture. These are additions to a well-built portfolio, not the foundation of one.

If you are a serious investor with ₹50 lakh+ in financial assets, already running a diversified equity MF portfolio, and looking for something that can protect and grow capital even when the Nifty drops 15% — then a Hybrid Long-Short or an Active Asset Allocator SIF deserves a serious look. Not as your core holding. As a satellite position, 5–15% of the portfolio at most.

Think of SIF as a well-equipped professional kitchen that has now opened its doors to serious home cooks. The equipment is excellent. The chefs are credentialed. But you are being asked to pay ₹10 lakh for a seat at a kitchen that has only cooked one season of meals. The menu looks extraordinary. Wait until you have tasted the monsoon.
