How Modes Are Assigned

Each ETF's weekly price is divided by the benchmark to get its relative strength. Two normalised readings are derived, both centred on 100:

  • RS-Ratio: is the ETF outperforming (>100) or underperforming (<100) the benchmark versus its own 10-week trend?
  • RS-Momentum: is that relative strength improving (>100) or fading (<100)?
◤ RecoveringRS-Ratio < 100 · RS-Momentum ≥ 100
▲ AcceleratingRS-Ratio ≥ 100 · RS-Momentum ≥ 100
▼ UnderperformingRS-Ratio < 100 · RS-Momentum < 100
◢ DeceleratingRS-Ratio ≥ 100 · RS-Momentum < 100

ETFs typically rotate clockwise: Recovering → Accelerating → Decelerating → Underperforming. Relative moves smaller than 1% are treated as noise, so ETFs that track the benchmark sit near the centre with low confidence.

What is an ETF?

An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a fund that holds a basket of investments — usually copying an index such as the Nifty 50, a sector, gold, silver, government bonds or a foreign index — and trades on the stock exchange like a share. One ETF unit gives you a slice of the whole basket. ETFs are regulated by SEBI, typically have very low expense ratios, and their holdings are disclosed regularly.

ETF, index fund or mutual fund — what is the difference?
  • ETF: bought and sold on the exchange at live prices through a demat account; usually the lowest cost; you pay brokerage and the bid-ask spread.
  • Index mutual fund: tracks the same index but is bought from the fund house at the day's NAV — no demat account needed and easy automatic SIPs, at a slightly higher cost.
  • Active mutual fund: a manager picks stocks aiming to beat the index, at a higher cost. See the India Mutual Fund Screener.
How do I buy an ETF?
  • Open a demat and trading account with KYC (see Best Brokers & Apps).
  • Search the ETF by its exchange symbol and check its liquidity (daily traded value) and expense ratio. Prefer ETFs that trade actively so you can buy and sell near their fair value.
  • Check the price against iNAV: the exchange publishes an indicative NAV during the day. Buy close to it; avoid paying a premium of more than about 0.5%.
  • Use a limit order (not a market order) during market hours (9:15 am – 3:30 pm on trading days), ideally avoiding the first and last 15 minutes when spreads are wider.
  • Invest regularly: buy a fixed amount every month on the same day, just as with a SIP.
Which ETF gives the maximum profit?

Honestly, nobody can know in advance — last year's top ETF is often not next year's. Sector and thematic ETFs can rise fastest but also fall hardest. What reliably improves your long-term result is:

  • A broad core — Nifty 50, Nifty Next 50, Nifty 500 or similar — for most of your money.
  • Low cost and tight tracking: lower expense ratio, small tracking error, and enough daily trading volume.
  • Smaller satellites (sector, factor such as momentum or value, international, gold) of 5–15% each, chosen with the modes on this page and your own research.
  • Time in the market: regular investing for many years matters more than picking the single best ETF.
When should I sell an ETF?
  • Core ETFs (broad market): hold for your goal's horizon; sell when you need the money, ideally in parts, or to rebalance once a year if equity has grown well above your target mix.
  • Satellite / momentum ETFs: decide your exit rule before you buy — for example, review when an ETF moves from Accelerating into Decelerating, and consider exiting if it slips into Underperforming for several weeks. Use a stop-loss you are comfortable with.
  • Sell near iNAV with a limit order, and remember tax: equity ETFs held more than 12 months are taxed at 12.5% on gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year, otherwise at 20%.
  • Don't sell in panic during a broad market fall — that is when regular buyers of core ETFs gain the most.

What is the best time to buy an ETF?

For core ETFs, the best time is regularly — a fixed amount every month on the same day — because nobody can time the market reliably. Extra money can be added when markets are clearly cheaper (for example after a fall of 10% or more, or when valuations are below their long-term average). Within a trading day, buy with a limit order close to iNAV, away from the opening and closing minutes. For satellite ETFs, the modes on this page help you judge timing (see the next question).

Accelerating, Decelerating, Recovering or Underperforming — which is best to buy?

The modes describe how an ETF has performed relative to the benchmark recently; they are a timing aid, not a guarantee.

  • ▲ Accelerating (outperforming and gaining strength): the strongest current trend — the usual choice for momentum buyers, but it can be late in the move, so buy in parts and watch for a shift to Decelerating.
  • ◤ Recovering (still behind, but improving): an early turn — potentially higher reward with higher risk, as some recoveries fade. Wait for confirmation (two or three weeks of improvement, rising volume) or buy a small first lot.
  • ▼ Decelerating (still ahead, but fading): a time to hold or book partial profits rather than add new money.
  • ◣ Underperforming (behind and weakening): avoid for momentum. Long-term value buyers may accumulate slowly, but only for ETFs they want to own for years.

A common rotation approach: add when an ETF moves from Recovering into Accelerating, hold while it stays Accelerating, and reduce when it turns Decelerating. Check the Confidence column, liquidity and premium to iNAV before acting. Broad core ETFs can be bought regularly whatever their mode.

What do the Trend S·M·L chips and the role tags mean?

Each ETF shows three trend chips from its weekly closing prices:

  • S — short term (1–3 months): price vs its 10-week average and the 13-week return.
  • M — medium term (6–12 months): price vs its 26-week average and the 6-month return.
  • L — long term (1–3 years): price vs its 52-week average, whether that average is rising, and the 1-year return.

▲ Positive = price above the average and rising over the period; ▼ Negative = below and falling; ● Neutral = mixed. Liquid ETFs show "Stable" because their price barely moves. Tap an ETF for the exact figures behind each label.

Roles show how an ETF is usually used in a portfolio: Core (broad Indian market — most of your long-term money), Satellite (sector, theme or factor — 5–15% each), Commodity (gold, silver), Debt (bonds, G-Secs, liquid), Global (international markets) and Hybrid (mixed assets). Use the Role and Trend filters above the list to narrow down. Trends describe past prices and can change quickly — they are an educational aid, not a buy or sell signal.

What are iNAV, premium and discount?

The iNAV (indicative NAV) is the live value of the ETF's holdings per unit, published during market hours. If the ETF's market price is above it, you pay a premium; below it, a discount. For liquid ETFs the gap is usually tiny; for thinly traded or international ETFs it can be several percent. Buy and sell close to iNAV with a limit order, and prefer ETFs with good daily trading volume.

How are ETFs taxed?
  • Equity ETFs (Indian shares): 20% on gains within 12 months; 12.5% on gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year after 12 months.
  • Gold, silver and international ETFs listed in India: taxed at your slab rate if held up to 12 months, and 12.5% on long-term gains after 12 months.
  • Debt ETFs (bonds, G-Secs, liquid): gains taxed at your slab rate however long you hold.

Surcharge and cess apply, and rules change with each Union Budget — please confirm with a tax professional.

How current is the data?

Prices are end-of-day NSE closes, refreshed after market close every trading day. The latest week includes trading up to the date shown at the top of the page. Newly listed ETFs are added automatically once they have enough trading history.

What does "Confidence" mean?

It cross-checks the mode against simple relative returns: whether the ETF has actually beaten the benchmark over 13 weeks, whether its recent relative return is improving, and whether it trades actively. High means all checks agree.

Why is an ETF "Not classified"?

At least 20 weeks of clean trading history are needed. Recently listed, suspended or rarely traded ETFs are listed but not classified, to avoid misleading signals.

How is the VilfinTV ETF rating calculated?

Every ETF with at least one year of trading history is compared only with similar ETFs: core (broad market, mid and small cap), satellite (sector, thematic and factor), global, hybrid, gold, silver, liquid and bond ETFs are ranked separately. Six parts are scored from 0 to 100 against those peers, then combined:

PartWeightWhat it measures
Cost20%Expense ratio — lower is better
Liquidity20%Average traded value per day — easier to buy and sell near fair value
Tracking & pricing15%Closing premium / discount to NAV and tracking error vs ETFs on the same index
Returns20%1-, 3- and 5-year returns
Risk15%Volatility, worst fall and return per unit of risk
Size & track record10%Fund size and years since listing

Stars follow a bell curve within each peer group: top 10% get ★★★★★, the next 22.5% ★★★★, the middle 35% ★★★, the next 22.5% ★★ and the bottom 10% ★. When a part is not available (for example an undisclosed expense ratio) the other weights are re-balanced. Very new or thinly traded ETFs show "Not rated". The rating is an educational comparison, not a recommendation — a 5-star sector ETF can still fall sharply if its sector does.

What is the VilfinTV scorecard (consistency, recency, volatility)?

The same scorecard as on our mutual fund pages, built from weekly ETF prices and compared within the same peer group:

  • Consistency (40%) — the share of month-ends over the last 3 (or 5) years when the ETF's rolling 1-year return was in the top half of its peers. High consistency means it did well again and again, not just once.
  • Recency (30%) — how its 3- and 6-month returns rank right now.
  • Low volatility (30%) — how calm its weekly returns were; steadier ETFs score higher.

Overall score = 40% consistency + 30% recency + 30% low volatility, with the rank among peers and the "Category leaders" table. Use the 3 years / 5 years toggle to compare horizons. Liquid and bond ETFs are not scored because their returns differ too little; ETFs with less than 3 years of prices show "Not scored".

What is Factor DNA?

Factor DNA shows what really drives an ETF's returns. Three years of its weekly returns are compared with ETFs tracking market (Nifty 50), mid cap, small cap, momentum, value, quality, low volatility, US and cash-like indices, and the closest mix is found (a method called returns-based style analysis). For example, a momentum ETF shows mostly "Momentum", while a Nifty 50 ETF shows almost 100% "Market".

"Explained" tells you how well that mix fits. Below about 60%, the ETF mostly follows its own sector or theme (IT, PSU, defence and so on), so treat the weights as a loose guide. Use Factor DNA to avoid owning several ETFs that are secretly the same bet.

Where do the ETF holdings come from?

Fund houses publish every scheme's full portfolio each month. When an ETF's own monthly portfolio is available, we show it ("Official monthly portfolio"). Most ETFs track an index, and every fund on the same index must hold the same stocks in the same weights — so when the ETF's own file is not readable yet, we show the official portfolio of the largest fund tracking the same index, clearly labelled "Index holdings" with the source fund named. Weights can differ slightly from the ETF's own (cash, timing). Portfolios are shown for up to 45 days after their month-end. Gold, silver, liquid and bond ETFs hold metal or debt instruments, not company shares, so no stock list is shown.

How do I read the trading chart?
  • Candles — each candle is one trading day: the thick body runs from the open to the close (green = closed higher than it opened, red = lower) and the thin wicks show the day's high and low. Switch to Line for a simple closing-price view.
  • Moving averages — the 20-day (short-term), 50-day (medium-term) and 200-day (long-term) averages smooth out daily noise. Price above a rising 200-day average is generally a healthy long-term trend; price below a falling one is a weak trend.
  • Volume — the bars at the bottom show how many units changed hands. Rising prices on rising volume are more convincing; very low volume means wider bid-ask spreads.
  • Prices are end-of-day closes. For live prices and the live iNAV during market hours, use the "Live quote & iNAV" link and buy with a limit order close to the iNAV.
How are returns, risk and fund facts calculated?

Returns up to one year use NSE closing prices. Three-year, five-year and since-listing returns are annualised and use each ETF's official NAV history, which is adjusted for unit splits. Volatility, beta and correlation use one year of daily prices; maximum drawdown covers three years. Expense ratio, fund size, NAV, benchmark and scheme documents come from official scheme disclosures. Premium / discount compares the closing price with that day's NAV — check the live iNAV on the exchange before trading.

Which benchmark should I use?

Nifty 50 suits most equity ETFs. Compare sector ETFs with their parent index (e.g. Bank ETFs vs Nifty Bank) to see whether they lead within their sector, and gold or silver ETFs vs Gold.
