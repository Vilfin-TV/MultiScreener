How ETF investing works — a beginner's guide

ETFs are bought and sold on the stock exchange, like shares, through a demat & trading account. The simulation assumes that on each investment day the investor checks the model portfolio and places a buy order for every ETF in it for the units shown. Nothing is bought automatically — you stay in control of every order.

  1. Open a demat & trading account (once)Keep your PAN, Aadhaar linked to your mobile number, a bank account in your own name and a cancelled cheque or bank statement ready. Account opening and KYC are online (e-sign with Aadhaar OTP) and usually take 1–3 working days. Choose a platform from Best Brokers & Apps, add a nominee, and enable the equity (cash) segment.
  2. Add money to your trading accountTransfer funds by UPI or net banking from your linked bank account before you buy. Purchases settle the next working day (T+1): the units appear in your demat account then.
  3. Fix your investment dayPick one day you will stick to — ideally 1–2 days after your salary arrives. Buy every ETF in the plan in the same session, so the portfolio keeps its intended mix.
  4. Place a limit order for each ETFSearch the exact NSE symbol shown in the plan, choose Buy · Delivery (CNC) — not intraday — enter the quantity in units from the plan and a limit price at or just above the current iNAV / best offer. Repeat for every ETF.

✅ Placing an order — checklist

  • Product type Delivery (CNC) — an intraday (MIS) order is squared off automatically the same day.
  • Use a limit order at or near the live iNAV (see "Live quote & iNAV" on each ETF). Avoid market orders, especially in ETFs with lower trading volume.
  • Trade between 9:30 am and 3:00 pm — spreads are widest in the first 15 minutes and the last few minutes of the session.
  • Check the bid-ask spread: under 0.3% for large ETFs is normal; a wide gap means you should wait or use a price closer to iNAV.
  • Enter whole units as shown; keep leftover cash for the next purchase.
  • Next day, check the contract note and that the units are in your demat holdings.

⛔ Common mistakes

  • Buying far above iNAV — global and gold ETFs sometimes trade at a premium in busy markets; the plan avoids ETFs trading far above NAV, but always check the live iNAV.
  • Market orders in thinly traded ETFs — you can pay much more than the fair value.
  • Selecting intraday instead of delivery.
  • Stopping in market falls — falls are when each rupee buys the most units.
  • Selling an ETF because it was replaced at a monthly review — existing units can stay; new money goes to the new ETF.
  • Owning several ETFs on the same index — it adds cost, not diversification.

🌍 For NRI investors (Non-Resident Indians)

Account & KYC

  • You need an NRI demat & trading account linked to an NRE account (repatriable — money can go back abroad) or an NRO account (non-repatriable). A resident demat account must be converted when you become an NRI.
  • Buying ETFs on the exchange from an NRE account needs PIS permission (Portfolio Investment Scheme) from your bank's designated branch; many platforms arrange this when the account is opened. NRO investing is usually allowed without PIS.
  • Documents: PAN, passport, visa / OCI card, overseas address proof, a photo and the FATCA / CRS declaration of your country of tax residence. Verification may need a video call or attested copies.
  • Not every platform onboards NRIs, and several do not accept residents of the USA or Canada — check before you apply. NRIs can buy and sell on delivery only (no intraday).

Tax for NRIs

  • Tax is deducted at source (TDS) by your broker / bank when you sell: equity ETFs 20% within 12 months and 12.5% after (gains above ₹1.25 lakh); gold, silver and international ETFs 12.5% after 12 months; debt ETFs and short-term gains taxed at the slab are usually deducted at 30% — plus surcharge and cess.
  • If more TDS is deducted than you owe, file an Indian income-tax return to claim a refund.
  • India's tax treaties (DTAA) can lower tax or give credit in your country of residence — keep a Tax Residency Certificate, Form 10F and your TDS certificates (Form 16A).
  • Sale proceeds go to the NRE / NRO account you invested from; NRE money can be repatriated freely. Confirm the details with a tax professional.

📉 Expect red years

  • Returns are not stable every year — equity ETFs can fall 10–20% in a normal year and far more in a crisis. The historical model estimate is a long-term average.
  • Stay invested for your full horizon and keep investing through falls; selling in a fall usually locks in the loss.
  • Need the money within 1–2 years? Move it gradually to the liquid ETF before then.

How Modes Are Assigned

Each ETF's weekly price is divided by the benchmark to get its relative strength. Two normalised readings are derived, both centred on 100:

  • RS-Ratio: is the ETF outperforming (>100) or underperforming (<100) the benchmark versus its own 10-week trend?
  • RS-Momentum: is that relative strength improving (>100) or fading (<100)?
◤ RecoveringRS-Ratio < 100 · RS-Momentum ≥ 100
▲ AcceleratingRS-Ratio ≥ 100 · RS-Momentum ≥ 100
▼ UnderperformingRS-Ratio < 100 · RS-Momentum < 100
◢ DeceleratingRS-Ratio ≥ 100 · RS-Momentum < 100

ETFs typically rotate clockwise: Recovering → Accelerating → Decelerating → Underperforming. Relative moves smaller than 1% are treated as noise, so ETFs that track the benchmark sit near the centre with low confidence.

  • Engine check — every momentum mode is recomputed with the exact calculation this page uses and must match.

What is an ETF?

An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a fund that holds a basket of investments — usually copying an index such as the Nifty 50, a sector, gold, silver, government bonds or a foreign index — and trades on the stock exchange like a share. One ETF unit gives you a slice of the whole basket. ETFs are regulated by SEBI, typically have very low expense ratios, and their holdings are disclosed regularly.

ETF, index fund or mutual fund — what is the difference?
  • ETF: bought and sold on the exchange at live prices through a demat account; usually the lowest cost; you pay brokerage and the bid-ask spread.
  • Index mutual fund: tracks the same index but is bought from the fund house at the day's NAV — no demat account needed and easy automatic SIPs, at a slightly higher cost.
  • Active mutual fund: a manager picks stocks aiming to beat the index, at a higher cost. See the India Mutual Fund Screener.
How do I buy an ETF?
  • Open a demat and trading account with KYC (see Best Brokers & Apps).
  • Search the ETF by its exchange symbol and check its liquidity (daily traded value) and expense ratio. Prefer ETFs that trade actively so you can buy and sell near their fair value.
  • Check the price against iNAV: the exchange publishes an indicative NAV during the day. Buy close to it; avoid paying a premium of more than about 0.5%.
  • Use a limit order (not a market order) during market hours (9:15 am – 3:30 pm on trading days), ideally avoiding the first and last 15 minutes when spreads are wider.
  • Invest regularly: buy a fixed amount every month on the same day, just as with a SIP.
Can anyone know which ETF will give the highest return?

Honestly, nobody can know in advance — last year's top ETF is often not next year's. Sector and thematic ETFs can rise fastest but also fall hardest. What reliably improves your long-term result is:

  • A broad core — Nifty 50, Nifty Next 50, Nifty 500 or similar — for most of your money.
  • Low cost and tight tracking: lower expense ratio, small tracking error, and enough daily trading volume.
  • Smaller satellites (sector, factor such as momentum or value, international, gold) of 5–15% each, chosen with the modes on this page and your own research.
  • Time in the market: regular investing for many years matters more than picking the single best ETF.
When should I sell an ETF?
  • Core ETFs (broad market): hold for your goal's horizon; sell when you need the money, ideally in parts, or to rebalance once a year if equity has grown well above your target mix.
  • Satellite / momentum ETFs: decide your exit rule before you buy — for example, review when an ETF moves from Accelerating into Decelerating, and consider exiting if it slips into Underperforming for several weeks. Use a stop-loss you are comfortable with.
  • Sell near iNAV with a limit order, and remember tax: equity ETFs held more than 12 months are taxed at 12.5% on gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year, otherwise at 20%.
  • Don't sell in panic during a broad market fall — that is when regular buyers of core ETFs gain the most.

What is the best time to buy an ETF?

For core ETFs, the best time is regularly — a fixed amount every month on the same day — because nobody can time the market reliably. Extra money can be added when markets are clearly cheaper — the simulator's market-dip lump sums use falls of 5%, 10% and 15% from the 3-month high with 37 checks (see the market-dip FAQs), or when valuations are below their long-term average. Within a trading day, buy with a limit order close to iNAV, away from the opening and closing minutes. For satellite ETFs, the modes on this page help you judge timing (see the next question).

Tracking error or tracking difference — what is the difference?

Tracking difference is how far the ETF's return was from its index over a period (for example, index +12.0%, ETF +11.7% → −0.3 percentage points); it mostly reflects the expense ratio and cash drag. Tracking error is how much that gap varies from week to week (the standard deviation of the return differences). A good index ETF has a small, steady tracking difference and a low tracking error. Both feed the "tracking & pricing" part of the VilfinTV rating.

There are many Nifty 50 ETFs — how do they differ?

They all hold the same 50 stocks, so returns differ only by small amounts. What matters is: liquidity (traded value per day — high liquidity means tight buy-sell spreads), price vs NAV (a small premium or discount), expense ratio, tracking difference / error and fund size. A cheaper ETF that trades rarely can cost more in spreads than it saves in fees. The VilfinTV rating and the Portfolio Simulator's ETF checks combine these factors; filter the table by benchmark Nifty 50 to compare them side by side.

Gold ETF, gold fund or Sovereign Gold Bonds?

Gold ETFs trade on the exchange through a demat account (no lock-in, small expense ratio). Gold funds are mutual funds that invest in gold ETFs — no demat needed, SIPs possible, slightly higher cost. Sovereign Gold Bonds paid 2.5% interest and were tax-free at maturity, but new issues have been paused since 2024; older bonds can still be bought on the exchange, where they often trade at a premium and with low liquidity. Gold ETFs held over 12 months are taxed at 12.5% on gains; gold funds after 24 months.

Accelerating, Decelerating, Recovering or Underperforming — what do they mean for me?

The modes describe how an ETF has performed relative to the benchmark recently; they are a timing aid, not a guarantee.

  • ▲ Accelerating (outperforming and gaining strength): the strongest current trend — the mode momentum investors usually focus on, though it can be late in the move; many spread purchases over time and watch for a shift to Decelerating.
  • ◤ Recovering (still behind, but improving): an early turn — potentially higher reward with higher risk, as some recoveries fade. Momentum investors often wait for confirmation (two or three weeks of improvement, rising volume).
  • ◢ Decelerating (still ahead, but fading): momentum investors often hold or trim here rather than add new money.
  • ▼ Underperforming (behind and weakening): the weakest mode for momentum. Long-term value investors sometimes accumulate slowly, but only ETFs they intend to hold for years.

A common rotation approach among momentum investors: add when an ETF moves from Recovering into Accelerating, hold while it stays Accelerating, and reduce when it turns Decelerating. Check the Confidence column, liquidity and premium to iNAV before acting. Broad core ETFs can be bought regularly whatever their mode.

What do the Trend S·M·L chips and the role tags mean?

Each ETF shows three trend chips from its weekly closing prices:

  • S — short term (1–3 months): price vs its 10-week average and the 13-week return.
  • M — medium term (6–12 months): price vs its 26-week average and the 6-month return.
  • L — long term (1–3 years): price vs its 52-week average, whether that average is rising, and the 1-year return.

▲ Positive = price above the average and rising over the period; ▼ Negative = below and falling; ● Neutral = mixed. Liquid ETFs show "Stable" because their price barely moves. Tap an ETF for the exact figures behind each label.

Roles show how an ETF is usually used in a portfolio: Core (broad Indian market — most of your long-term money), Satellite (sector, theme or factor — 5–15% each), Commodity (gold, silver), Debt (bonds, G-Secs, liquid), Global (international markets) and Hybrid (mixed assets). Use the Role and Trend filters above the list to narrow down. Trends describe past prices and can change quickly — they are an educational aid, not a buy or sell signal.

What are iNAV, premium and discount?

The iNAV (indicative NAV) is the live value of the ETF's holdings per unit, published during market hours. If the ETF's market price is above it, you pay a premium; below it, a discount. For liquid ETFs the gap is usually tiny; for thinly traded or international ETFs it can be several percent. Buy and sell close to iNAV with a limit order, and prefer ETFs with good daily trading volume.

How are ETFs taxed?
  • Equity ETFs (Indian shares): 20% on gains within 12 months; 12.5% on gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year after 12 months.
  • Gold, silver and international ETFs listed in India: taxed at your slab rate if held up to 12 months, and 12.5% on long-term gains after 12 months.
  • Debt ETFs (bonds, G-Secs, liquid): gains taxed at your slab rate however long you hold.

Surcharge and cess apply, and rules change with each Union Budget — please confirm with a tax professional.

How current is the data?

Prices are end-of-day NSE closes, refreshed after market close every trading day. The latest week includes trading up to the date shown at the top of the page. Newly listed ETFs are added automatically once they have enough trading history.

What does "Confidence" mean?

It cross-checks the mode against simple relative returns: whether the ETF has actually beaten the benchmark over 13 weeks, whether its recent relative return is improving, and whether it trades actively. High means all checks agree.

Why is an ETF "Not classified"?

At least 20 weeks of clean trading history are needed. Recently listed, suspended or rarely traded ETFs are listed but not classified, to avoid misleading signals.

Why doesn't the Portfolio Simulator show the ETFs with the very highest returns?

Because the ETFs at the top of last year's return table are rarely the ones at the top next year, and they usually carry the most risk. The simulator is built to be held through good and bad years, so it deliberately avoids chasing them:

  • Concentration: the highest recent returns almost always come from one sector, theme or style (for example PSU, defence, small caps or a single overseas market) that has just had an unusually strong run. One theme can also fall the hardest.
  • Valuation: after a big run that segment is usually expensive. The simulator's valuation gates halve or pause equity segments above the 80th / 90th percentile of their own 10-year history.
  • Leaders rotate: sector and style leadership changes every few years, so a top-ranked ETF often slips to average or below — the screener's rolling returns and calendar-year tables show this clearly.
  • Bigger falls: high-return ETFs typically have higher volatility and deeper drawdowns. The Risk & score tab shows how much a mix can fall; a concentrated portfolio can fall far more.
  • Checks and caps: every ETF must pass checks on cost, size, track record, consistency (not just one good year), risk and drawdown, and growth or sector sleeves are capped at 10–15% of a mix.

You can still explore every ETF and its returns in the screener — the simulator simply shows what a diversified, rules-based mix looks like. Chasing the latest winners is one of the most common ways investors lose money.

Why does the ETF Portfolio Simulator rarely show international ETFs?

Indian ETFs that track overseas markets (for example the Nasdaq 100 or S&P 500) can only create new units within an overseas investment limit set for the whole industry (about US$1 billion for overseas ETFs, within a wider limit of about US$7 billion for mutual funds). Once a fund house uses its share, it cannot issue new units, so buyers on the exchange bid the price up and these ETFs often trade 5–20% above their NAV. Buying at such a premium means paying more than the holdings are worth, and the premium can vanish suddenly when limits are eased. The simulator therefore includes a global ETF only when its price is known and within ±2% of NAV; otherwise it explains why and keeps that money in Indian ETFs. GIFT City funds and US-listed ETFs bought through an international account are other routes abroad (taxed differently).

I have extra money this month (or week) — which ETF should it go into?

The simulator cannot tell you what to buy, but these are the rules it follows itself: keep an emergency fund and clear expensive debt first; keep money needed within 3 years in a liquid ETF or liquid fund; for long-term money, either add it in the same proportions as the model portfolio (choose One-time and enter the amount — the page converts it into whole units), or add it to the ETF that is furthest below its target weight after market moves, which rebalances without selling. For a large sum while the market is expensive, the guide's liquid-ETF approach moves it into equity ETFs over 6–12 months. Money kept aside for market falls can follow the Market-dip lump sums section (₹5,000 / ₹10,000 / ₹20,000 at 5% / 10% / 15% falls held for two closes, after 37 checks). Avoid putting it all into the ETF that rose the most recently. This is educational information, not a recommendation for your situation.

What are market-dip lump sums?

A rule-based extra one-time purchase the model shows when a market segment falls. For each segment — large cap (Nifty 50), mid cap (Nifty Midcap 150), small cap (Nifty Smallcap 250), gold and silver — the official close is compared with the highest close of the last 3 months (63 trading sessions):

Fall from the 3-month highModel dip amount
5% or more₹5,000
10% or more₹10,000
15% or more₹20,000

Only the first close at a deeper tier counts, so a fall gives at most one buy per tier (5% → 10% → 15%). The fall ends when the index closes back within 1% of its high, and the tiers start again. The amount goes into the segment's ETF in the model portfolio you selected — a segment that is not in that plan has no dip amount. Minimum ₹5,000, maximum ₹20,000. The section is shown in the Portfolio Simulator, just above Simulator Buy.

What are the 37 checks before a dip lump sum?

Every check must pass on the day:

  • Market trigger (1–8): a fall of at least 5% from the 3-month high; an up-to-date official close; the fall held for two closes (a one-day dip that recovers the next session never counts); the fall confirmed by a second ETF on the same index (within 1.5 points); not a crash-day print (the day's move not worse than −8%); the fall within 40% (a bigger gap points to bad data); a level and amount reached; and a new level in this fall.
  • Market & valuation (9–22): the model is not pausing the segment; P/E below the 80th percentile of 10 years and within 1.15× its 10-year median; P/B below its 80th percentile; earnings yield not far below the 10-year government bond yield; 12-month earnings not falling more than 15%; the ETF tracks the segment's index; the Nifty 50 below its 80th valuation percentile; no panic selling (the last 5 sessions not down more than 12%); the long-term trend intact (no more than 30% below the 52-week high); the economy & earnings checks not negative overall; no falling streak (no more than 7 of the last 10 closes lower than the day before); the 200-day average still rising; and global market fear not extreme (volatility index at or below 35). Gold and silver use their own checks instead of P/E: below the 90th percentile of 2 years, no more than 15% above the 200-day average, the model's metal choice, a 12-month trend not broken, a US dollar that is not surging, plus a rupee that is not surging against the dollar (USD/INR not down more than 4% in 3 months), no falling streak, a rising 200-day average and a US 10-year yield that has not jumped more than 0.75 point in 3 months.
  • ETF (23–37): the model's choice for the segment and passing all of its own slot checks, traded in each of the last 20 sessions, at least ₹1 crore traded a day, price within ±0.75% of NAV, expense ratio within its cost cap, tracking score of at least 50, fund size of at least ₹100 crore, at least a year listed, a VilfinTV rating of 3★ or more, one unit costing ₹5,000 or less, tracking error no more than 1.25× the median of ETFs on the same index, a 1-year tracking difference within ±1%, an up-to-date NAV, and an amount between ₹5,000 and ₹20,000.

Expand any segment in the Market-dip lump sums section to see each check with its figure. Why does the dip rule start at 5% and not 3%?

Falls of 3% from a 3-month high are common — several a year in every segment — and in VilfinTV's 5-year review of weekly closes they were usually followed by ordinary returns: for the Nifty 50, an average of about +1% over the next 6 months after a 3% dip, against about +6% after a 5% dip and +7% after a 10% dip (Nifty Midcap 150: about +7%, +10% and +15%). Starting at 3% spent the reserve on small wobbles and left little for real corrections. From 5%, the amounts rise to ₹10,000 at 10% and ₹20,000 at 15%, so most of the money is kept for deeper, better-value falls. Past figures over a short period are an illustration only — they do not predict future returns.

Is every dip bought?

No. A fall alone is never enough. Each dip lump sum needs all 37 checks to pass on the day, the fall must hold for two closes, and the value checks are deliberately strict: the segment's P/E must be below the 80th percentile of 10 years and within 1.15× its 10-year median, the Nifty 50 below its 80th percentile, earnings must not be collapsing, and the fund or ETF must pass its own quality checks. A fall in an expensive market, a fall the second index tracker does not confirm, a crash-day print or a segment outside your model portfolio is shown with the reason and no amount.

What if the market falls one day and recovers the next?

Then nothing is bought. A level counts only when two consecutive closes are at or beyond it — the first close at 5% shows "waits for a second close", and if the index bounces back the next session the signal disappears and the ETF shows No dip buy today. The model also skips a fall when the last 5 sessions dropped more than 12% (panic selling) or when the index is more than 30% below its 52-week high, and it waits a session after a crash-day print. Dip signals count only from the Simulator Buy start on 19 Oct 2026 — a fall that began earlier is shown, but its levels are not bought; the next level reached after the start can be.

The market fell — why is there no dip amount today?

Common reasons: the fall is under 5% from the 3-month high (a fall from an older high does not count); that tier was already used earlier in the same fall — the next amount comes only at a deeper tier; the fall is not yet confirmed by the second ETF, or the day itself was a crash-day print (it waits a session); valuations are still too high (P/E checks); the segment's ETF failed a check; or the segment is not part of the model portfolio you selected. The status column shows which applies.

Should I invest on dips instead of investing regularly?

No. Dip lump sums are an extra for money kept aside, never a replacement for the regular plan. Falls of 5% happen once or twice a year in most segments and nobody knows whether a fall will stop at 5% or reach 30%, so the rule only adds modest amounts in steps. Keep your emergency fund and regular investments first. Money waiting for a dip sits in a liquid ETF, where it earns a liquid-fund return taxed at your slab when withdrawn. Historical figures show that staying invested matters far more than timing a fall. This is educational information, not advice.

Can I do an SWP or tax harvesting with ETFs?

SWP: ETFs have no automatic withdrawal plan — you sell units yourself on the exchange (delivery sale, money in your bank account the next working day). Many investors keep one to two years of withdrawals in a liquid ETF or liquid fund and refill it once a year from the equity ETFs, which works like the bucket method described in the mutual fund FAQs. Tax harvesting: works the same as with mutual funds — long-term gains (units held more than 12 months) of up to ₹1.25 lakh a year on equity ETFs are tax-free, so selling units with up to that much gain and buying them back resets your cost higher and can save up to about ₹16,250 a year per person (12.5% + 4% cess); losses can be booked to set off other gains. Remember brokerage, the bid-ask spread, STT and stamp duty on each trade. Confirm with a tax professional.

Why are health insurance and term life insurance usually advised before investing in mutual funds or ETFs?

Because insurance protects your plan and investments grow it — without protection, one bad event can force you to sell your investments at the worst time. Most financial planners suggest this order:

  1. Emergency fund — 6 months of expenses in a savings account, liquid or money-market fund.
  2. Health insurance — a family floater for you, your spouse and children, and separate cover for parents if possible. Hospital bills can run into lakhs and medical costs rise about 10–14% a year; without cover, a single hospital stay could wipe out years of SIPs. An employer's group policy usually ends when you change or lose your job, so a personal policy (bought young, while healthy, to avoid waiting periods and exclusions) is safer. A low-cost super top-up adds a large cover above a deductible.
  3. Term life insurance — if anyone depends on your income (spouse, children, parents, a home loan), a pure term plan pays a lump sum to them if you die during the term. A common guide is 10–15 times your annual income plus outstanding loans, to around retirement age. It is far cheaper than endowment, money-back or ULIP plans because it carries no investment component. If no one depends on your income, you may not need it yet.
  4. Clear expensive debt (credit cards, personal loans).
  5. Then invest for each goal in mutual funds or ETFs.

Keep insurance and investment separate: term + health insurance for protection and mutual funds / ETFs for growth usually gives far more cover and better long-term growth than plans that mix the two. Under the old tax regime, health premiums can qualify under Section 80D and term premiums under Section 80C. This is general education, not insurance advice — compare policies' claim-settlement records, waiting periods, room-rent limits and exclusions, and buy from an IRDAI-registered insurer or intermediary.

I am now an NRI — can I keep investing through my resident bank account and resident KYC?

No. Under India's foreign-exchange rules (FEMA), once you become a non-resident Indian you must tell your bank, and your resident savings account has to be re-designated as an NRO account without delay. You must also update your status to NRI with your KYC registration, every fund house, your demat / broker account and your investment platform. Continuing to invest as a "resident" after becoming an NRI is a violation and can lead to penalties, frozen accounts or problems when you redeem.

  • Why it matters for tax too: NRIs have tax deducted at source when they redeem (for example 12.5% on long-term and 20% on short-term equity gains), different rules for some debt funds, and treaty (DTAA) benefits that only work when your status is correct.
  • How NRIs invest: through an NRE account (money earned abroad — investments and gains can be taken back abroad) or an NRO account (income earned in India — repatriation is limited). Many fund houses accept NRIs, but some do not accept investors resident in the US or Canada. ETFs and shares need an NRI demat and trading account, and NRE-funded trades go through a Portfolio Investment Scheme (PIS) account.
  • Existing investments: you do not need to sell them. Update the status on each folio and demat account and link your NRO (or NRE) bank account for redemptions.
My wife is also an NRI but still has a resident bank account — can I give her money to invest in mutual funds or ETFs?

You can give your spouse money, but two things have to be right first:

  • Her status must be correct: if she lives abroad and qualifies as an NRI, her resident savings account must also be converted to NRO, and her KYC and investment accounts updated to NRI — the same rule as for you. She cannot invest as a resident while she is an NRI.
  • Gifting is allowed: you may transfer money to her NRE / NRO account (a gift between spouses is not taxed as income in her hands).
  • But the income is "clubbed" with yours: under Indian tax law, gains and income from investments bought with money you gave your spouse are added to your taxable income — so this does not lower the family's tax. It works only for convenience or estate planning, not tax saving. (If she invests her own earnings, the income is hers.)
  • Simpler options: invest in your own name with her as a joint holder or nominee, or each invest your own money from your own NRE / NRO accounts.

Rules differ by country of residence (for example US or Canada) — please confirm with a tax professional familiar with NRI taxation.

Can I, as an NRI, invest through my parents' resident account instead?

Only as a genuine gift, and it is usually not a good idea as a way around NRI rules.

  • A real gift is legal: an NRI may gift money to resident relatives such as parents from an NRE or NRO account, and the gift is not taxed in their hands. The investments then legally belong to your parents: they decide what to do with them, gains are taxed in their names (they get their own ₹1.25 lakh yearly long-term gain exemption and their own slab), and on their death the investments pass under their will or succession law — possibly shared with siblings, not automatically back to you.
  • A front is not: using a parent's name and account while you keep control of the money and treat it as yours is risky — it can be treated as a benami arrangement or a breach of foreign-exchange rules, and causes disputes later.
  • Better routes: invest in your own name as an NRI (NRE for repatriable money, NRO for Indian income) and add your parents as nominees; or, if you want to support them, gift the money openly so it is theirs to invest.
Should I simply not convert my account to NRI and keep buying from my resident account?

No — this is not allowed and not worth the risk. Keeping a resident account and investing as a resident after you become an NRI breaks foreign-exchange rules, can attract penalties, and creates trouble later: redemptions may be held up, KYC mismatches surface when a fund house or bank checks your address, PAN or tax filings, and tax deducted may be wrong. Converting is usually simple — inform your bank (resident account → NRO), update KYC to NRI with your passport, visa / overseas address proof and PAN, and change the status on each fund house, demat and investment-platform account. When you return to India for good, do the reverse: re-designate your accounts as resident (you may first be treated as "resident but not ordinarily resident" for tax).

This is general information, not legal or tax advice — please check the latest rules with your bank and a tax professional.

What is a "historical model estimate"?

It is the yearly return the simulator's rules assume for each ETF and for the mix, worked out from historical returns, today's valuations and bond yields, after expenses. It is a long-term model figure used to draw the illustration — not a forecast, target or promise. Actual returns will be different and will vary a lot from year to year, including years with losses.

What are futures and options (F&O), and why are they not for beginners?

Futures and options are derivative contracts on an index (Nifty, Bank Nifty) or a stock. They are leveraged — a small margin controls a large position — and they expire, so a small market move can wipe out the money put in, and futures or sold options can lose more than that. SEBI's 2024 study found that about 93% of individual F&O traders lost money between FY2022 and FY2024, with an average loss of around ₹2 lakh per trader. SEBI has since raised the minimum contract size (₹15 lakh+), limited weekly expiries to one benchmark index per exchange and tightened margins to protect small investors. For long-term wealth, a low-cost, diversified ETF portfolio held for years is far more reliable; learn derivatives only with money you can afford to lose.

Can I protect my ETF portfolio with options? What is hedging?

Hedging means taking a position that gains when your portfolio loses. With options, the usual tools are a protective put on the Nifty 50 (pays off if the index falls below the strike — the premium is the cost of the "insurance" and is lost if the market does not fall), a collar (buy a put, sell a call to pay for it — limits both loss and gain) and covered calls (sell calls against holdings for income — caps upside, little crash protection). Contract sizes are large (₹15 lakh+ per lot), the hedge must match your portfolio's value and beta, and repeated hedging costs a lot over time. For most investors, simpler risk management works better and costs nothing: the right mix of equity, debt and gold (as in the Portfolio Simulator), an emergency fund so you never sell in a crash, diversification and yearly rebalancing.

What is algo trading (algorithmic trading)?

Algo trading means software places orders automatically when pre-set rules are met, instead of a person clicking each order. Institutions use it to split large orders and trade efficiently. SEBI's framework for retail algo trading (rolled out from 2025) allows individuals to run algorithms only through their broker's approved channel: each algo order is tagged for the exchange, algo providers must be empanelled with the exchanges, and API access is controlled by the broker. Be careful: back-tested strategies often fail in live markets, errors can place many wrong orders quickly, and SEBI repeatedly warns about apps and Telegram groups promising guaranteed returns from "algos". VilfinTV does not offer algo trading — the screener and simulator only calculate and rank using published rules; they never place orders.

How is the VilfinTV ETF rating calculated?

Every ETF with at least one year of trading history is compared only with similar ETFs: core (broad market, mid and small cap), satellite (sector, thematic and factor), global, hybrid, gold, silver, liquid and bond ETFs are ranked separately. Six parts are scored from 0 to 100 against those peers, then combined:

PartWeightWhat it measures
Cost20%Expense ratio — lower is better
Liquidity20%Average traded value per day — easier to buy and sell near fair value
Tracking & pricing15%Closing premium / discount to NAV and tracking error vs ETFs on the same index
Returns20%1-, 3- and 5-year returns
Risk15%Volatility, worst fall and return per unit of risk
Size & track record10%Fund size and years since listing

Stars follow a bell curve within each peer group: top 10% get ★★★★★, the next 22.5% ★★★★, the middle 35% ★★★, the next 22.5% ★★ and the bottom 10% ★. When a part is not available (for example an undisclosed expense ratio) the other weights are re-balanced. Very new or thinly traded ETFs show "Not rated". The rating is an educational comparison, not a recommendation — a 5-star sector ETF can still fall sharply if its sector does.

What is the VilfinTV scorecard (consistency, recency, volatility)?

The same scorecard as on our mutual fund pages, built from weekly ETF prices and compared within the same peer group:

  • Consistency (40%) — the share of month-ends over the last 3 (or 5) years when the ETF's rolling 1-year return was in the top half of its peers. High consistency means it did well again and again, not just once.
  • Recency (30%) — how its 3- and 6-month returns rank right now.
  • Low volatility (30%) — how calm its weekly returns were; steadier ETFs score higher.

Overall score = 40% consistency + 30% recency + 30% low volatility, with the rank among peers and the "Category leaders" table. Use the 3 years / 5 years toggle to compare horizons. Liquid and bond ETFs are not scored because their returns differ too little; ETFs with less than 3 years of prices show "Not scored".

What is Factor DNA?

Factor DNA shows what really drives an ETF's returns. Three years of its weekly returns are compared with ETFs tracking market (Nifty 50), mid cap, small cap, momentum, value, quality, low volatility, US and cash-like indices, and the closest mix is found (a method called returns-based style analysis). For example, a momentum ETF shows mostly "Momentum", while a Nifty 50 ETF shows almost 100% "Market".

"Explained" tells you how well that mix fits. Below about 60%, the ETF mostly follows its own sector or theme (IT, PSU, defence and so on), so treat the weights as a loose guide. Use Factor DNA to avoid owning several ETFs that are secretly the same bet.

Where do the ETF holdings come from?

Fund houses publish every scheme's full portfolio each month. When an ETF's own monthly portfolio is available, we show it ("Official monthly portfolio"). Most ETFs track an index, and every fund on the same index must hold the same stocks in the same weights — so when the ETF's own file is not readable yet, we show the official portfolio of the largest fund tracking the same index, clearly labelled "Index holdings" with the source fund named. Weights can differ slightly from the ETF's own (cash, timing). Portfolios are shown for up to 45 days after their month-end. Gold, silver, liquid and bond ETFs hold metal or debt instruments, not company shares, so no stock list is shown.

How do I read the trading chart?
  • Candles — each candle is one trading day: the thick body runs from the open to the close (green = closed higher than it opened, red = lower) and the thin wicks show the day's high and low. Switch to Line for a simple closing-price view.
  • Moving averages — the 20-day (short-term), 50-day (medium-term) and 200-day (long-term) averages smooth out daily noise. Price above a rising 200-day average is generally a healthy long-term trend; price below a falling one is a weak trend.
  • Volume — the bars at the bottom show how many units changed hands. Rising prices on rising volume are more convincing; very low volume means wider bid-ask spreads.
  • Prices are end-of-day closes. For live prices and the live iNAV during market hours, use the "Live quote & iNAV" link and buy with a limit order close to the iNAV.
How are returns, risk and fund facts calculated?

Returns up to one year use NSE closing prices. Three-year, five-year and since-listing returns are annualised and use each ETF's official NAV history, which is adjusted for unit splits. Volatility, beta and correlation use one year of daily prices; maximum drawdown covers three years. Expense ratio, fund size, NAV, benchmark and scheme documents come from official scheme disclosures. Premium / discount compares the closing price with that day's NAV — check the live iNAV on the exchange before trading.

Which benchmark should I use?

Nifty 50 suits most equity ETFs. Compare sector ETFs with their parent index (e.g. Bank ETFs vs Nifty Bank) to see whether they lead within their sector, and gold or silver ETFs vs Gold.
