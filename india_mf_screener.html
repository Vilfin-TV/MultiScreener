How mutual fund investing works — a beginner's guide

The Portfolio Simulator is an educational model — it never invests for you. If you decide to invest on your own, you carry out every purchase yourself. The simulation assumes that on each investment day the investor checks the model portfolio and buys every fund in it as a one-time (lump-sum) purchase for the amount shown in the plan table. It is not an automatic SIP — nothing is debited from your bank account unless you place the orders. Buying this way means each new instalment always follows the latest monthly review of the plan, and every fund in it accepts one-time purchases from ₹1,000 or less.

  1. Complete your KYC (once)Keep your PAN card, Aadhaar (linked to your mobile number) and a bank account in your own name ready. KYC is done online in about 10 minutes when you open an investment account and usually becomes active within 1–3 working days. Your KYC status should show Validated or Registered before you invest.
  2. Open a direct mutual fund accountChoose a platform from Best Brokers & Apps below. Choose one that offers Direct plans: there is no distributor commission, so you keep more of your returns. Add your bank account and a nominee.
  3. Fix your investment dayPick one day you will stick to — ideally 1–2 days after your salary arrives, so the money is invested before it is spent. See the timing tips below for your chosen way of investing.
  4. Buy each fund in the planSearch the exact fund name, choose Direct – Growth, select One-time / Lump sum, enter the amount shown in the plan and pay by UPI or net banking from your own bank account. Repeat for every fund in the plan.

✅ Placing an order — checklist

  • Pick the Direct plan and the Growth option — not Regular, not IDCW.
  • Pay from your own bank account (UPI / net banking). Third-party payments are rejected.
  • Place orders on a market working day before 2:00 pm so the payment reaches the fund house before its 3:00 pm cut-off and you get that day's price (NAV). Orders after the cut-off or on holidays get the next working day's price.
  • Units appear in your account in 1–3 working days. Check every fund in the plan was bought.
  • Keep a simple record of what you bought and when (your platform's portfolio page shows this too).

⛔ Common beginner mistakes

  • Stopping when markets fall — falls are when each rupee buys the most units.
  • Selling a fund because it was replaced — existing units can stay; selling may cost exit load and tax. Only new money goes to the new fund.
  • Chasing last year's top fund or adding many extra funds — the plan is already diversified.
  • Investing money you need soon — keep 6 months of expenses as an emergency fund first.
  • Skipping months — regular investing matters far more than the perfect day.

🌍 For NRI investors (Non-Resident Indians)

KYC & account

  • Documents: PAN, passport, overseas address proof (utility bill, bank statement or residence permit), a recent photo, and visa / OCI card where applicable.
  • Declare your residential status as NRI and your country of tax residence (FATCA / CRS form). If you were already KYC-registered as a resident, update your status to NRI before investing.
  • Invest only from an NRE account (money can be taken back abroad) or an NRO account (non-repatriable). A resident savings account or a foreign bank account cannot be used.
  • Not every platform supports NRI accounts — check before you sign up. Some fund houses do not accept residents of the USA and Canada, and others ask for an extra declaration; confirm that every fund in your plan accepts you before placing orders.
  • Verification may need a video call or attested copies, so allow a few extra working days.

Tax

  • Gains are taxed in India at the same rates as for residents, but the fund house deducts tax (TDS) when you sell: on equity funds 20% on short-term gains (held up to 12 months) and 12.5% on long-term gains; on debt funds bought after 1 April 2023, 30%. Surcharge and 4% cess are added.
  • Long-term gains of up to ₹1.25 lakh a year on equity funds are tax-free. If more TDS is deducted than you owe, file an Indian income-tax return to get a refund.
  • India has tax treaties (DTAA) with most countries. Your country of residence may also tax the gains, usually with credit for tax paid in India — keep your TDS certificates (Form 16A).
  • Redemption money is credited to the NRE / NRO account you invested from. The projection on this page uses resident tax rules — the final tax is similar, but TDS reduces the cash you receive until any refund. Rates change with each Union Budget — please confirm with a tax professional.

📉 Expect red years

  • Returns are not stable every year — equity funds can fall 10–20% in a normal year and far more in a crisis. The historical model estimate is a long-term average.
  • Stay invested for your full investment horizon and keep investing through falls; selling in a fall usually locks in the loss.
  • Need the money within 1–2 years? Move it gradually to a liquid or arbitrage fund before then.

How to read this screener
  • Returns up to 1 year are absolute; 3, 5 and 10 year returns are per year, compounded (CAGR) — marked p.a.. Since-launch returns start from the launch of the Direct plan and are per year once the fund is more than a year old.
  • SIP returns assume ₹10,000 invested on the same date every month and are annualised (XIRR).
  • Rolling returns show every possible holding period — the best, the worst and how often the fund beat its benchmark. They reveal consistency that point-to-point returns hide.
  • Risk ratios use 36 monthly returns to the last month-end. Higher Sharpe / Sortino means more return per unit of risk; a downside capture below 100 means the fund fell less than its benchmark in falling months.
  • Factor DNA shows which investment style best explains an equity fund's behaviour — Momentum, Value, Quality or Low Volatility — together with its market-cap tilt.
  • VilfinTV Rating ranks funds within their category on risk-adjusted return, downside protection, long-term return and cost. Funds need a 3-year record to be rated. Sectoral and thematic funds are grouped by theme (banking, pharma, technology, infrastructure and more) and rated against all sectoral / thematic funds. Index funds are rated only against funds tracking the same index — tracking error 35%, tracking difference 25%, expense ratio 25%, fund size 10%, track record 5% — and need 1+ year of history and at least 3 funds on that index.

What is a mutual fund?

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests it in a mix of shares, bonds, gold or other assets, managed by a professional fund manager and regulated by SEBI. When you invest you receive units; the value of one unit is the NAV (net asset value), published every business day. If the investments grow, the NAV rises and so does the value of your units. Your money is held in your name by an independent trustee and custodian — not by the fund house — and you can usually withdraw any business day.

  • Equity funds invest mainly in shares — for goals 5+ years away.
  • Debt funds invest in bonds and deposits — for stability and short-term goals.
  • Hybrid funds mix both; index funds simply copy an index such as the Nifty 50 at low cost.
Why invest through mutual funds?
  • Start small: from ₹100–₹1,000 a month — no need to wait until you have a large sum.
  • Diversification: one fund holds 30–100+ companies, so one company's problems don't sink your savings.
  • Professional management and regulation: SEBI rules on disclosure, costs and the safe keeping of your money.
  • Beats inflation over time: equity funds have historically grown faster than prices, which is what builds real wealth.
  • Liquidity: withdraw on any business day; money reaches your bank in 1–3 working days.
  • Transparency and tax efficiency: daily NAV, monthly portfolios, and gains are taxed only when you sell.

The trade-off: returns are not guaranteed and values go up and down, so equity funds suit money you can leave invested for at least 5 years.

Direct plan or Regular plan — which should I choose?

Every fund has two versions holding the same portfolio. A Regular plan pays a commission to the distributor out of your money every year; a Direct plan does not, so its expense ratio is lower — often by 0.5–1.5% a year for equity funds. Over 20 years that difference can add up to a large share of your final amount. This screener shows Direct plans only. Choose Direct plans through a direct mutual fund platform (see Best Brokers & Apps), and choose the Growth option so returns keep compounding.

Index fund or actively managed fund?

An index fund copies an index (such as the Nifty 50) at very low cost — you get the market's return, minus a small fee, with no risk of a fund manager making poor choices. An active fund tries to beat its index through stock selection, at a higher cost; some do over long periods, many do not. A sensible approach for most investors: an index fund as the core, plus a few consistent active funds (check rolling returns, consistency and the VilfinTV score on each fund) for mid- and small-cap exposure, where good managers have more room to add value.

Why not FDs or ULIP / insurance plans for long-term wealth?

They have their place, but for goals 7+ years away they usually grow your money much less.

  • Fixed deposits: safe and predictable, but interest is taxed every year at your slab. A 7% FD earns about 4.9% after tax in the 30% slab — close to inflation, so your money barely grows in real terms. Example: ₹1,000 a month for 20 years at 4.9% after tax ≈ ₹4 lakh, versus ≈ ₹9.2 lakh at an illustrative 12% from equity funds. FDs remain ideal for emergency money and goals within 1–3 years.
  • ULIPs and endowment / money-back insurance plans: they mix insurance with investment. Charges (premium allocation, policy administration, mortality and fund management) reduce returns, especially in early years; money is locked in for at least 5 years; endowment plans often return only about 4–6% a year; and ULIP maturity is tax-free only if annual premiums stay within ₹2.5 lakh.
  • A better combination: buy a pure term insurance plan (large cover at low cost) plus health insurance, and invest the rest in mutual funds. You get more protection and usually much more wealth — and each part can be changed independently.
Real estate or mutual funds — which builds more wealth?

Both can, but they behave very differently.

  • Amount needed: property needs lakhs upfront and usually a loan; mutual funds start at a few hundred rupees.
  • Returns: residential rent is typically only 2–3% of the property's value a year; price growth varies widely by city and is uneven. Diversified equity funds have historically delivered higher long-term returns, though with ups and downs along the way.
  • Costs: stamp duty and registration (often 5–8%), brokerage, maintenance, property tax and loan interest — versus an expense ratio below 1% a year for direct funds.
  • Liquidity: selling a property can take months; fund units can be sold any business day, even in part.
  • Concentration: one property is one asset in one location; one fund spreads money across dozens of companies.

Buying a home to live in is a lifestyle decision as much as an investment. For pure wealth building, regular investing in mutual funds is simpler, cheaper and more flexible; many families do both — a home to live in, and mutual funds for growth.

Yes. Starting early and staying regular matters far more than the amount. Illustration at 12% a year (returns are not guaranteed and vary year to year):

  • Step up every year: raise the amount by 10% each year (₹1,000 becomes ₹1,100, then ₹1,210 …). Over 20 years that turns about ₹6.9 lakh invested into roughly ₹18.6 lakh at 12% — about twice the result of a fixed ₹1,000.

  • Protect it: keep a small emergency fund first, never stop during market falls, and do not withdraw for non-goal spending.
How many mutual funds should I own?

For most people 3 to 5 funds are enough: for example an index or large-cap fund, a flexi-cap fund, a mid- or small-cap fund for long horizons, a debt or hybrid fund for stability, and optionally gold. Owning 10–15 funds rarely adds diversification — they end up holding the same stocks (check overlap in Compare) and become hard to track. The Portfolio Simulator plans use up to eight funds because they also cover debt, gold and international markets.

Mutual funds or ETFs — what is the difference?

An ETF is a fund that trades on the stock exchange like a share, at live prices during market hours, and needs a demat account; a mutual fund is bought from the fund house at the day's NAV, with no demat needed and easy automatic SIPs. ETFs usually have the lowest costs; index mutual funds are simpler for regular monthly investing. Compare NSE ETFs on ETF Momentum Mode.

Is the data on this site accurate?

We work hard to keep it accurate, but please treat it as a research aid — not the final word.

  • Official sources: NAVs, fund details, expense ratios, minimum amounts and exit loads come from official industry and fund-house disclosures; portfolios come from each fund house's statutory monthly portfolio statement.
  • Automated checks every day: every return is recalculated independently and reconciled against the official NAV history; unit splits and one-day price errors are corrected; portfolios are checked to add up and to fit the fund's category. If a check fails, the update is not published and the previous data stays.
  • Timing: NAVs and returns refresh every business day, usually by the next morning; risk ratios and rolling returns use the last month-end; portfolios are published by fund houses up to 10 days after month-end and are shown for at most 45 days. Portfolios are not yet available for every fund house.
  • Limits: fund houses can change expense ratios, exit loads, minimum amounts or purchase status at short notice, and a source can occasionally contain errors. Past returns, ratings and plans are calculations from historical data, not predictions.

Always confirm the latest details on the fund house's website and in the scheme documents before you invest.

What should I check before buying a fund?

A short checklist before every first purchase:

  • You: an emergency fund of 6 months' expenses is in place; the money will not be needed before the fund's horizon (5+ years for equity); KYC is active; you understand the risk level and are ready for falls.
  • The fund on its official website: the exact scheme name, Direct plan – Growth option; that it is open for new purchases; the latest expense ratio, exit load and minimum amount; the riskometer and category; the fund manager.
  • The documents: read the Key Information Memorandum (KIM) and the latest factsheet — investment objective, benchmark, portfolio and risks. The Scheme Information Document (SID) has the full details.
  • Fit with what you hold: avoid owning several funds of the same category or with heavily overlapping portfolios (use Compare on this site); check how the fund changes your mix of equity, debt and gold.
  • Tax & costs: how gains will be taxed when you sell, any exit load if you might need the money early, and that you are buying through a direct (commission-free) route.
  • When unsure: consult a SEBI-registered investment adviser. VilfinTV is an educational platform and does not give investment advice.
Which funds are covered?

Every open-ended Indian mutual fund scheme in its Direct plan, Growth option — equity, debt, hybrid, commodity, international, index and solution-oriented funds. ETFs are covered separately in ETF Momentum Mode.

How are categories assigned?

Funds follow their official SEBI category. Fund-of-funds and index funds are further grouped by what they actually hold: gold and silver funds under Commodity, overseas funds under Global, debt index and target-maturity funds under Debt, and passive equity funds under Index.

When is the data updated?

Every morning, once fund houses have published the previous business day's NAV. Risk ratios, rolling returns and Factor DNA are computed as of the last month-end, the convention used in fund factsheets.

Which benchmark is used?

Equity funds are compared with the index matching their category (Nifty 100 for large caps, Nifty Midcap 150, Nifty Smallcap 250, Nifty LargeMidcap 250, otherwise Nifty 500). Benchmark returns include dividends: each index is tracked through its longest-running Direct-plan index fund, so the comparison is like-for-like after costs. Hybrid funds show equity sensitivity against the Nifty 50. Debt, commodity and international funds are compared with their category peers.

Are 3-year and 5-year returns per year? What is CAGR (compounding)?

Yes — every return longer than one year on this site is a compounded annual return (CAGR), i.e. the steady yearly rate that would turn the starting value into the ending value. Returns of up to one year (1D, 1W, 1M, 3M, 6M, YTD, 1Y) are absolute — the plain change over that period. "Since launch" is per year (CAGR) once the fund is more than a year old. Tables mark these as p.a. (per year) or absolute.

Formula: CAGR = (ending value ÷ starting value)1 ÷ years − 1. Each year's gain earns gains of its own in the following years — that is compounding.

₹1,00,000 at 12% a year (CAGR)ValueTotal gain
After 1 year₹1,12,00012%
After 3 years₹1,40,49340%
After 5 years₹1,76,23476%
After 10 years₹3,10,585211%
  • A 5-year CAGR of 12% is a 76% total gain, not 60% (12 × 5) — compounding adds the extra 16%.
  • The reverse also holds: a 76% total gain over 5 years is 12% a year, not 15.2% (76 ÷ 5).
  • Rule of 72: money roughly doubles in 72 ÷ yearly return years — about 6 years at 12%, 10 years at 7%.
  • CAGR is a smoothed average: the actual yearly returns go up and down (see Calendar-year returns on each fund).
  • SIP returns use XIRR, the compounded yearly return that accounts for each instalment's own date, since each instalment is invested for a different length of time.
  • Portfolio Simulator projections compound each fund's historical model estimate a year every month on every instalment until the end of your holding period, then deduct tax.
How is the risk-free rate chosen?

Sharpe and Sortino ratios subtract the overnight money-market return for the same months, so they adapt automatically as interest rates change.

What do the scores and status mean?

Consistency is the share of the last 36 month-ends in which the fund ranked in the top half of its category on rolling 1-year return. Recency blends 3-month, 6-month and 1-year returns (20/30/50) into a category percentile. Volatility ranks 3-year volatility within the category (100 = calmest). Status: In form — recency in the top 30%; On track — recency between the 40th and 70th percentile; Underperforming — recency in the bottom 40% but a top-half long-term record; Out of track — both recent and long-term returns in the bottom half. Index funds are not scored on these, since they track an index by design — instead they get a VilfinTV rating against the other funds tracking the same index (how closely they follow it, cost, size and track record) and an Index tracking card.

How is taxation shown?

Each fund is mapped to its capital-gains treatment from its SEBI category and stated asset allocation: equity-oriented, specified (debt-oriented), or other. Rates exclude surcharge and cess and may change with each Union Budget — please confirm with a tax professional.

Where do portfolio holdings come from?

From each fund house's statutory monthly portfolio disclosure, published within ten days of every month-end. The Portfolio tab shows the top holdings, sector allocation, asset mix and market-cap split as of that month-end. Market-cap buckets follow SEBI's ranking — Nifty 100 constituents are large cap, Nifty Midcap 150 constituents are mid cap and other listed stocks are small cap. Portfolios are being added fund house by fund house.

Can I invest a lump sum, or quarterly / yearly?

Yes. Under How you invest, choose Monthly, Weekly, Quarterly, Yearly or One-time. The funds and weights are the same for every option, so the plan stays consistent however you invest; only the amounts, the projection and the tax change. For a one-time amount the planner adds an entry plan: when the Nifty 50 is fairly valued (below the 60th percentile of its 10-year valuation) everything is invested at once; when it is expensive, the equity share is parked in a liquid fund and moved in monthly over 6 months (12 months above the 80th percentile), using a systematic transfer plan within the same fund house where possible. Debt, hybrid and gold allocations are invested on day one.

How does the Portfolio Simulator work?

Choose your investment horizon and risk level, and the planner builds a diversified plan of up to eight funds across equity, hybrid, debt and gold — with small thematic and international satellites only for aggressive plans of seven years or more. The market is falling and my portfolio is in the red. Should I sell?

Usually no — but here is the honest picture so you can decide for yourself.

  • Falls are normal, not a sign the plan failed.   • Selling turns a temporary fall into a permanent loss. Investors who sell in a fall often miss the recovery, because the best days frequently come soon after the worst.
  • Keep investing if you can. Your regular investments buy more units when prices are low, which is what makes the long-term average work.
  • Stick to your investment horizon. Judge the plan over the period you chose (5, 7 or 10+ years), not over a few months. Equity money should be money you will not need for at least 5 years.

Only the first close at a deeper tier counts, so a fall gives at most one buy per tier (3% → 5% → 10%). The fall ends when the index closes back within 1% of its high, and the tiers start again. The amount goes into the segment's fund in the model portfolio you selected — a segment that is not in that plan has no dip amount. Minimum ₹5,000, maximum ₹20,000. What are the 26 checks before a dip lump sum?

Every check must pass on the day:

  • Market trigger (1–7): fall of at least 3% from the 3-month high; an up-to-date official close; the fall confirmed by a second tracker on the same index (within 1.5 points); not a crash-day print (the day's move not worse than −8%); the fall within 40% (a bigger gap points to bad data); a tier and amount reached; and a new tier in this fall.
  • Valuation (8–14): the model is not pausing the segment for valuation; P/E below the 90th percentile of 10 years and within 1.25× its 10-year median; earnings yield not far below the 10-year government bond yield; 12-month earnings not falling more than 15%; the fund's portfolio P/E is below 1.25× its category median (index funds, and funds without a published portfolio, are valued by their index); and the Nifty 50 not above its 90th valuation percentile. For gold and silver: price below the 95th percentile of 2 years, no more than 20% above its 200-day average, the model's metal choice and a 12-month trend not broken.
  • Fund (15–26): the model's choice for the segment and passing all of its own slot checks, open for lump-sum purchases, a lump-sum minimum of ₹5,000 or less, exit load of 1% or less and within 12 months, expense ratio at or below the category median, fund size of at least ₹500 crore, a 3-year track record, a 3-year return no more than 1 point below the category median, a worst fall no more than 5 points deeper than the category median, a fund status that is not lagging, and an amount between ₹5,000 and ₹20,000.

Expand any segment in the Market-dip lump sums section to see each check with its figure.

What is an SWP, and which withdrawal method suits me?

A Systematic Withdrawal Plan (SWP) sells units from a fund on a fixed date every month (or quarter) and pays the money to your bank — the reverse of a SIP. It suits retirement income, or paying for a goal over several years from money already invested. There are several ways to set it up:

  • 1. Fixed-amount SWP: the same amount every month (for example ₹40,000). Simple and predictable; the risk is selling more units when markets are down.
  • 2. Fixed-percentage SWP: withdraw a set share of the current value each year (for example 4–5%), recalculated once a year. Income rises after good years and dips after bad ones, but the money lasts much longer.
  • 3. Gains-only (appreciation) SWP: withdraw only the growth above your invested amount, which some fund houses offer as an option. Your capital stays intact, but income is irregular and stops in falling markets.
  • 4. Bucket method: split the money into three: Bucket 1 — 1–2 years of withdrawals in a liquid, money-market or arbitrage fund (the SWP runs from here); Bucket 2 — the next 3–7 years in hybrid, balanced-advantage or high-quality debt funds; Bucket 3 — the rest in diversified equity for growth. Once a year refill Bucket 1 from whichever bucket has done well, so you never have to sell equity after a fall.
  • 5. Inflation step-up SWP: start lower (3.5–4% of the corpus a year) and raise the amount by about 5–6% each year so your spending power stays the same.
  • 6. Tax-aware SWP: withdraw from equity-oriented funds held more than 12 months — only the gain part of each withdrawal is taxed, and long-term gains up to ₹1.25 lakh a year are tax-free. Example: ₹50 lakh in a fund bought for ₹30 lakh; a ₹3 lakh withdrawal contains about ₹1.2 lakh of gain, so it can be tax-free. Income from IDCW (dividend) options and FD interest is taxed in full at your slab instead.

What is tax harvesting, and how much tax can it really save each year?

Two legal ways to lower the tax on your mutual fund gains, done before 31 March every financial year (current rules: equity-oriented funds — 12.5% on long-term gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year, 20% on short-term gains within 12 months; debt funds bought after 1 April 2023 — your slab rate).

  • Gain harvesting (equity-oriented funds): each year, sell units whose long-term gain adds up to about ₹1.25 lakh and reinvest the money in the same fund the same or next day. That gain is tax-free, and your purchase price resets higher, so less gain is taxed when you finally sell. Saving: up to ₹15,625 a year (₹16,250 with cess) per person. Done every year for 20 years, that is over ₹3 lakh of tax avoided, plus growth on it.
  • Use every family member's limit: the ₹1.25 lakh exemption is per person (and per HUF). Investments held in a spouse's or parent's name from their own money each get their own exemption — but money gifted to a spouse or minor child is "clubbed" back to you, so their gains count as yours.
  • Loss harvesting: sell a fund that is below your cost to book a capital loss, then buy a similar (or the same) fund back. Short-term losses can be set off against both short- and long-term gains; long-term losses only against long-term gains. Unused losses carry forward for 8 years — only if you file your income-tax return by the due date. Losses cannot be set off against salary.
  • Order of work in March: (1) download your capital-gains statement for the year; (2) book any losses first; (3) then harvest long-term gains up to the remaining ₹1.25 lakh; (4) reinvest the same day or the next working day to stay invested.
  • Rules and costs to check: units are sold first-in, first-out, so make sure the units sold are older than 12 months and past any exit load; ELSS units cannot be sold within their 3-year lock-in; each purchase pays 0.005% stamp duty and each equity-fund sale 0.001% STT; you are out of the market for a day or two; the new units start a fresh 12-month holding period. India currently has no "wash-sale" rule, but keep the reinvestment genuine.
  • Debt funds: gains are taxed at your slab and there is no yearly exemption, so gain harvesting does not help; loss harvesting still does.

What is DTAA and how does it help NRIs?

A Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement (DTAA) is a tax treaty between India and another country (India has treaties with about 90 countries) that stops the same income from being fully taxed twice. For NRIs investing in Indian mutual funds: the fund house deducts TDS in India when you sell; your country of residence may tax the same gain but usually gives you a credit for the tax paid in India. Under some treaties, mutual fund gains may be taxed at a lower rate or only in your country of residence. To use a treaty, obtain a Tax Residency Certificate (TRC) from your country of residence and file Form 10F in India; submit them to the fund house before you redeem so TDS follows the treaty rate, or claim the difference as a refund in your Indian income-tax return. Keep your TDS certificates (Form 16A) for the foreign tax return. Treaty terms differ by country — please confirm with a tax professional.

I am now an NRI — can I keep investing through my resident bank account and resident KYC?

No. Under India's foreign-exchange rules (FEMA), once you become a non-resident Indian you must tell your bank, and your resident savings account has to be re-designated as an NRO account without delay. You must also update your status to NRI with your KYC registration, every fund house, your demat / broker account and your investment platform. Continuing to invest as a "resident" after becoming an NRI is a violation and can lead to penalties, frozen accounts or problems when you redeem.

  • Why it matters for tax too: NRIs have tax deducted at source when they redeem (for example 12.5% on long-term and 20% on short-term equity gains), different rules for some debt funds, and treaty (DTAA) benefits that only work when your status is correct.
  • How NRIs invest: through an NRE account (money earned abroad — investments and gains can be taken back abroad) or an NRO account (income earned in India — repatriation is limited). Many fund houses accept NRIs, but some do not accept investors resident in the US or Canada. ETFs and shares need an NRI demat and trading account, and NRE-funded trades go through a Portfolio Investment Scheme (PIS) account.
  • Existing investments: you do not need to sell them. Update the status on each folio and demat account and link your NRO (or NRE) bank account for redemptions.
My wife is also an NRI but still has a resident bank account — can I give her money to invest in mutual funds or ETFs?

You can give your spouse money, but two things have to be right first:

  • Her status must be correct: if she lives abroad and qualifies as an NRI, her resident savings account must also be converted to NRO, and her KYC and investment accounts updated to NRI — the same rule as for you. She cannot invest as a resident while she is an NRI.
  • Gifting is allowed: you may transfer money to her NRE / NRO account (a gift between spouses is not taxed as income in her hands).
  • But the income is "clubbed" with yours: under Indian tax law, gains and income from investments bought with money you gave your spouse are added to your taxable income — so this does not lower the family's tax. It works only for convenience or estate planning, not tax saving. (If she invests her own earnings, the income is hers.)
  • Simpler options: invest in your own name with her as a joint holder or nominee, or each invest your own money from your own NRE / NRO accounts.

Rules differ by country of residence (for example US or Canada) — please confirm with a tax professional familiar with NRI taxation.

Can I, as an NRI, invest through my parents' resident account instead?

Only as a genuine gift, and it is usually not a good idea as a way around NRI rules.

  • A real gift is legal: an NRI may gift money to resident relatives such as parents from an NRE or NRO account, and the gift is not taxed in their hands. The investments then legally belong to your parents: they decide what to do with them, gains are taxed in their names (they get their own ₹1.25 lakh yearly long-term gain exemption and their own slab), and on their death the investments pass under their will or succession law — possibly shared with siblings, not automatically back to you.
  • A front is not: using a parent's name and account while you keep control of the money and treat it as yours is risky — it can be treated as a benami arrangement or a breach of foreign-exchange rules, and causes disputes later.
  • Better routes: invest in your own name as an NRI (NRE for repatriable money, NRO for Indian income) and add your parents as nominees; or, if you want to support them, gift the money openly so it is theirs to invest.
Should I simply not convert my account to NRI and keep buying from my resident account?

No — this is not allowed and not worth the risk. Keeping a resident account and investing as a resident after you become an NRI breaks foreign-exchange rules, can attract penalties, and creates trouble later: redemptions may be held up, KYC mismatches surface when a fund house or bank checks your address, PAN or tax filings, and tax deducted may be wrong. Converting is usually simple — inform your bank (resident account → NRO), update KYC to NRI with your passport, visa / overseas address proof and PAN, and change the status on each fund house, demat and investment-platform account. When you return to India for good, do the reverse: re-designate your accounts as resident (you may first be treated as "resident but not ordinarily resident" for tax).

This is general information, not legal or tax advice — please check the latest rules with your bank and a tax professional.

When should I add international funds, and how can I invest abroad?

Consider putting 10–20% of your long-term portfolio outside India once it reaches a meaningful size (for example ₹10 lakh or more), and direct more new money abroad when Indian shares look expensive — the Portfolio Simulator shows the Nifty 50 valuation percentile, and its model moves more new money abroad above the 80th percentile. International holdings spread risk across other economies and gain when the rupee weakens.

  • Indian international funds: simplest, in rupees — but often closed to new money when the industry-wide overseas limit is reached (see the purchase-status filter).
  • GIFT City (IFSC) funds: dollar funds set up in India's international financial centre, outside that limit; invested under the RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) (up to USD 250,000 a year), usually with a higher minimum.
  • US-listed ETFs: broad S&P 500, Nasdaq 100 or world-market ETFs bought through an international investing account — for example Dhan, listed under Best Brokers & Apps — also under LRS.
  • Tax & rules: remittances above ₹10 lakh a year attract 20% tax collected at source (TCS), adjustable against your income tax; gains are taxed at 12.5% if held more than 24 months, otherwise at your slab; US dividends have 25% US tax withheld (claim credit with Form 67); report foreign assets in your income-tax return (Schedule FA); US-listed holdings above USD 60,000 may face US estate tax — GIFT City funds or Ireland-domiciled ETFs avoid this.

Confirm current rules with a tax professional.

Can NRIs invest, and how does KYC work for them?

Yes. Non-Resident Indians complete KYC with PAN, passport, overseas address proof and a FATCA / CRS declaration of their country of tax residence, and invest from an NRE (repatriable) or NRO (non-repatriable) bank account — not from a resident savings account or a foreign bank account. If you were registered as a resident, update your KYC status to NRI first. Not every platform supports NRI accounts, and some fund houses do not accept residents of the USA and Canada (others need an extra declaration), so check each fund in your plan before investing.

How are NRIs taxed on mutual fund gains?

Gains are taxed at the same rates as for residents, but tax is deducted at source (TDS) when you sell: 20% on short-term equity gains (held up to 12 months), 12.5% on long-term equity gains, and 30% on gains from debt funds bought after 1 April 2023, plus surcharge and cess. Long-term equity gains of up to ₹1.25 lakh a year are exempt; any excess TDS can be claimed back by filing an Indian income-tax return. Under India's tax treaties (DTAA), your country of residence usually gives credit for tax paid in India. Rates change with each Union Budget — please confirm with a tax professional.

What does “Fresh investment paused” mean?

Some funds — mostly overseas funds when the industry-wide overseas investment limit is reached — temporarily stop accepting new lump-sum purchases or SIPs. Existing investments are unaffected. Use the purchase-status filter in Explore to see which global funds are open for investment today.

Where do new fund offers come from?

The NFO tab lists schemes in their official new-fund-offer period, with offer dates, minimum investment, category and links to the offer document and fund house. Recently closed offers stay listed for 45 days.

Is a new fund at ₹10 NAV cheaper than an old fund at ₹500? (the "₹10 NFO" myth)

No. The NAV is just the value of one unit; it says nothing about whether a fund is cheap or expensive. What matters is how the fund's holdings grow — and a percentage rise is the same whatever the unit price.

  • How it is mis-sold: new fund offers are sometimes pitched as "buy at ₹10 before the price goes up", "like an IPO", "limited period", or as a reason to sell an existing good fund and switch. A mutual fund is not an IPO — there is no listing gain, and a high old NAV only means the fund has grown well for years.
  • Why the push: fund houses spend on marketing a new launch, and distributors earn commission on Regular plans; frequent switching also earns them fresh commission. SEBI has repeatedly warned against mis-selling and "churning" investors between schemes.
  • Protect yourself: buy the Direct plan yourself, ask "what does this fund offer that existing funds do not?", read the scheme information document, and never switch out of a fund only to buy an NFO — it can also trigger tax and exit loads.
Is there any advantage in buying a fund in its NFO? What are the disadvantages?

Usually little advantage, and some real disadvantages. A new fund is bought at the same value as its holdings — there is no discount for being early.

Possible advantagesDisadvantages and risks
Access to something genuinely new — a strategy, index or asset class no existing fund offers.No track record: you cannot see how the manager handles a fall, how closely an index fund tracks, or its real costs.
A low-cost index fund or ETF from a large, reliable fund house can be as good as an old one from day one.Launch timing: fund houses tend to launch what is popular — themes and sectors after a strong run, often near expensive valuations.
Closed-ended or interval funds can be bought only during their offer.Money waits: it sits idle during the offer period and is invested over the following weeks.

A common rule of thumb is to wait 1–3 years and compare the fund with established peers, unless it offers something you cannot get elsewhere. For index NFOs, existing funds on the same index with a proven tracking record are usually just as good. This is general information, not a recommendation about any particular offer.

Is it risky to invest with a small fund house?

Your money is not at risk because a fund house is small. In India a mutual fund is set up as a trust: your investments are held by an independent custodian in the scheme's name, separate from the fund house's own money, and overseen by trustees and SEBI. If a fund house closes or is sold, its schemes are taken over by another fund house or wound up and paid out to investors — the fund house's own losses cannot be charged to you.

  • Real risks to check: smaller research teams; key-person risk (one star manager leaving can change a fund); fewer funds to switch between within the same house; and in small debt funds, a few large redemptions can force sales at poor prices.
  • Possible advantages: a smaller, focused fund house can be nimble, and a smaller small-cap or mid-cap fund can buy and sell its stocks more easily.
  • What to look at: at least 3–5 years of track record through a market fall, the manager's tenure, consistency vs the category (the scorecard and Fund health on each fund page), fund size, portfolio quality (credit ratings for debt funds) and costs. The Portfolio Simulator also requires a minimum fund size and track record for every fund.
Is there any problem with very large funds (high AUM)?

It depends on what the fund buys:

  • Large-cap, flexi-cap and index funds, liquid and high-quality debt funds: size is generally an advantage — costs fall (expense-ratio limits step down as a fund grows), trading costs are spread, index funds usually track more tightly, and debt funds are more diversified and less affected by redemptions.
  • Small-cap and mid-cap funds: very large size can be a drag. Small companies have limited shares available, so a big fund either owns many more stocks (becoming more like an index), drifts towards larger companies, or moves prices against itself when buying or selling. This is why some large small-cap funds have paused or limited lump-sum investments, and why SEBI asks these funds to publish stress tests — how many days it would take to sell 25% or 50% of the portfolio.
  • Thematic and sector funds: a fund that grew very large after a hot run may simply reflect money chasing past returns.
  • How the simulator handles it: small-cap funds must stay within a manageable size, and every fund needs a minimum size — so both very small and over-sized small-cap funds are avoided.
Why are health insurance and term life insurance usually advised before investing in mutual funds?

Because insurance protects your plan and investments grow it — without protection, one bad event can force you to sell your investments at the worst time. Most financial planners suggest this order:

  1. Emergency fund — 6 months of expenses in a savings account, liquid or money-market fund.
  2. Health insurance — a family floater for you, your spouse and children, and separate cover for parents if possible. Hospital bills can run into lakhs and medical costs rise about 10–14% a year; without cover, a single hospital stay could wipe out years of SIPs. An employer's group policy usually ends when you change or lose your job, so a personal policy (bought young, while healthy, to avoid waiting periods and exclusions) is safer. A low-cost super top-up adds a large cover above a deductible.
  3. Term life insurance — if anyone depends on your income (spouse, children, parents, a home loan), a pure term plan pays a lump sum to them if you die during the term. A common guide is 10–15 times your annual income plus outstanding loans, to around retirement age. It is far cheaper than endowment, money-back or ULIP plans because it carries no investment component. If no one depends on your income, you may not need it yet.
  4. Clear expensive debt (credit cards, personal loans).
  5. Then invest for each goal in mutual funds.

Keep insurance and investment separate: term + health insurance for protection and mutual funds for growth usually gives far more cover and better long-term growth than plans that mix the two. Under the old tax regime, health premiums can qualify under Section 80D and term premiums under Section 80C. This is general education, not insurance advice — compare policies' claim-settlement records, waiting periods, room-rent limits and exclusions, and buy from an IRDAI-registered insurer or intermediary.

How is Factor DNA calculated?

Three years of weekly returns are matched against NSE's Momentum, Value, Quality, Low-Volatility and size indices. The best-fitting combination (weights that are positive and add to 100%) shows the fund's style. A fund is labelled with a single style only when that style dominates and the fit is strong; otherwise it is a Blend.
